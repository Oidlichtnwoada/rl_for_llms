\abstract{This seminar report critically reviews the work "LaSeR: Reinforcement Learning with Last-Token Self-Rewarding" by Yang et al. (2025) [cite_start][cite: 1663, 1664]. The study addresses the inefficiency of current self-verification methods in Large Language Models (LLMs) by theoretically deriving that the optimal reasoning reward is equivalent to the model's last-token self-rewarding score. [cite_start]By augmenting standard Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) with a mean squared error loss on the last token's log-probability, LaSeR jointly optimizes reasoning and verification capabilities[cite: 1671, 1672]. [cite_start]The findings demonstrate that this approach achieves high reasoning accuracy and self-verification performance on mathematical benchmarks with negligible inference cost[cite: 1675]. Strengths of the paper include its elegant theoretical foundation and significant efficiency gains. [cite_start]However, limitations exist regarding its generalization to non-mathematical domains and reliance on specific prompt structures[cite: 2092]. This report is organized into four sections: a story summary, a paper summary, a critical quality analysis, and an overall conclusion.}

\section{Story summary}
\label{sec:storysummary}

\subsection*{1. What is the central question?}
[cite_start]The paper addresses a critical efficiency bottleneck in Large Language Models (LLMs): How can we unify reasoning and self-verification capabilities within a single model to provide reliable signals at test time, without the high computational cost of external verifiers or sequential self-correction?[cite: 1668, 1670, 1686].

\subsection*{2. Why is this question important?}
[cite_start]Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) relies on ground-truth answers which are unavailable during test-time inference[cite: 1682]. [cite_start]While existing solutions like external verifiers or self-correction prompts exist, they are inefficient: external verifiers require training separate models, and sequential self-verification doubles the inference cost[cite: 1684, 1685]. [cite_start]Solving this allows for efficient "test-time scaling" where models can self-select correct answers with minimal overhead[cite: 1732].

\subsection*{3. What evidence/data (variables) are needed to answer this question?}
[cite_start]The authors need to measure reasoning accuracy (e.g., Pass@1), self-verification performance (F1 score), and computational cost (inference tokens)[cite: 1896, 1897, 1728]. [cite_start]Additionally, they must validate the theoretical correlation between the "last-token self-rewarding score" (a log-probability ratio) and the true verifier reward[cite: 1726].

\subsection*{4. What methods are used to get this evidence/data?}
Yang et al. (2025) [cite_start]propose LaSeR, which adds a Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss to the standard RLVR objective, aligning the model's last-token log-probability for a specific token (e.g., \texttt{<vision\_start>}) with the true correctness of the solution[cite: 1672, 1720]. [cite_start]They compare this against baselines like GRPO and external Reward Models (PRMs/ORMs) using LLaMA-3.2 and Qwen-2.5 architectures[cite: 1881, 1916].

\subsection*{5. What analyses must be applied for the data to answer the central question?}
[cite_start]To validate the method, the authors must demonstrate that the simplified theoretical assumption (that the partition function $Z(x,y) \approx 1$) holds empirically[cite: 1806]. [cite_start]They must also analyze the correlation between the learned self-rewarding scores and ground truth correctness across different mathematical benchmarks (MATH500, AIME, etc.)[cite: 1889].

\subsection*{6. What evidence/data (values for the variables) were obtained?}
[cite_start]LaSeR achieved reasoning accuracy comparable to or better than standard GRPO (e.g., 80.2\% vs 79.9\% on MATH500 for Qwen2.5-7B)[cite: 1885]. [cite_start]Crucially, it achieved high self-verification F1 scores (approx. 80\%) [cite: 1914][cite_start], matching the performance of a 72B external reward model while requiring only one additional token generation per solution[cite: 1917, 1728].

\subsection*{7. What were the results of the analyses?}
[cite_start]The analysis confirmed that the "last-token self-rewarding score" effectively captures solution correctness, allowing the model to act as its own verifier[cite: 1901]. [cite_start]The simplification of the reference model probability to a constant was empirically validated, further reducing computational overhead[cite: 1827]. [cite_start]However, generalization to general reasoning tasks (like MMLU-Pro) was less effective than in the math domain[cite: 2090].

\subsection*{8. How did the analyses answer the central question?}
[cite_start]The results proved that reasoning and verification can be jointly optimized without significant overhead[cite: 1672]. [cite_start]By encoding the verification signal into the last token's probability distribution, LaSeR eliminates the need for a second inference pass, successfully unifying the generator and verifier[cite: 1746].

\subsection*{9. What does this answer tell us about the broader field?}
[cite_start]This implies that LLMs possess latent self-evaluation capabilities that can be unlocked via specific RL objectives without complex architectural changes or massive external reward models[cite: 1675]. [cite_start]It suggests a path toward more efficient "System 2" reasoning where models inherently know when they are correct[cite: 1732].

\subsection*{10. Did the paper answer the question satisfactorily? Why (not)?}
[cite_start]Yes, the paper satisfactorily answered the question by providing a rigorous theoretical derivation and backing it with extensive empirical results on standard benchmarks[cite: 1671, 1885]. [cite_start]The method's efficiency (one extra token) directly addresses the central problem of computational cost in self-verification[cite: 1728].

\section{Paper Summary}
\label{sec:papersummary}

This chapter summarizes the paper "LaSeR: Reinforcement Learning with Last-Token Self-Rewarding" by Yang et al. (2025) [cite_start]\cite{yang2025laser}[cite: 1663]. The summary is structured to reflect the logical flow of a machine learning research paper, covering the introduction, methodology, experimental setup, and results.

\subsection{Introduction}
[cite_start]Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has become a cornerstone for improving the reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs), as exemplified by systems like OpenAI o1 and DeepSeek-R1 \cite{guo2025deepseek}[cite: 1680]. By rewarding reasoning chains based on final answer correctness, RLVR encourages models to develop robust problem-solving strategies. [cite_start]However, a major limitation of standard RLVR is its reliance on ground-truth labels, which are unavailable during test-time inference[cite: 1682].

[cite_start]To mitigate this, prior works have utilized external verifiers or joint optimization strategies where the model generates a solution and then a separate verification output \cite{lightman2023lets}[cite: 1683]. Yang et al. (2025) [cite_start]argue that these approaches are inefficient: external verifiers require training and hosting separate models, while sequential self-verification doubles the inference cost by requiring a second generation pass[cite: 1684, 1685]. [cite_start]The authors introduce LaSeR to unify reasoning and verification within a single model with minimal overhead[cite: 1686].

\subsection{Methods}
\textbf{Theoretical Framework.} The authors derive their method from the RL objective of verification. They theoretically demonstrate that the optimal verification reward $\hat{r}(x,y,z)$ has a closed-form solution. [cite_start]Specifically, the true reasoning reward $r_v(x,y)$ is equivalent to the log-probability ratio between the policy model $\pi_\theta$ and the reference model $\pi_{ref}$ for a specific token $z_c$ (e.g., "Yes" or a special token) at the end of the solution[cite: 1814]:
\begin{equation}
	r_v(x,y) = \beta_v \log \left[ \frac{\pi_\theta(z_c|x,y)}{\pi_{ref}(z_c|x,y)} \right]
\end{equation}
[cite_start]A key insight supporting this derivation is that the partition function $Z(x,y)$, usually a complex term in such derivations, approximates to 1 because the probability of the reference model predicting specific verification tokens is negligibly small[cite: 1808].

[cite_start]\textbf{LaSeR Algorithm.} Based on this insight, LaSeR augments the standard RLVR loss (optimized via algorithms like GRPO) with an additional Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss[cite: 1724]. [cite_start]This loss aligns the "last-token self-rewarding score" (the calculated log-probability difference) with the actual verifier reward derived from ground truth[cite: 1725].
[cite_start]This forces the model to embed its confidence about the solution into the probability distribution of the final token[cite: 1687]. [cite_start]To handle class imbalance between correct and incorrect solutions during training, the authors apply a dynamic re-weighting strategy to the loss function[cite: 1833].

\textbf{Efficiency.} A crucial methodological contribution is the simplification of the reference model term. [cite_start]The authors observe that $\log \pi_{ref}(z_c|x,y)$ is effectively constant across inputs[cite: 1827]. [cite_start]By replacing it with a pre-calculated constant $c_{ref}$, LaSeR eliminates the need for a forward pass through the reference model during self-rewarding calculation, further enhancing efficiency[cite: 1828].

\subsection{Experiments}
[cite_start]\textbf{Setup.} The authors evaluated LaSeR using LLaMA-3.2 (3B) and Qwen-2.5 (7B) architectures[cite: 1881]. [cite_start]The models were trained on the DeepMath-103K dataset \cite{he2025deepmath}[cite: 1888]. [cite_start]Evaluation was conducted on five mathematical reasoning benchmarks: MATH500, AMC23, AIME24, AIME25, and OlympiadBench[cite: 1889].

[cite_start]\textbf{Baselines.} The method was compared against standard pre-trained base models, models trained with standard GRPO (without self-rewarding), and solutions using external Reward Models (ORMs and PRMs)[cite: 1881, 1916].

[cite_start]\textbf{Metrics.} The primary metrics were Pass@1 accuracy for reasoning performance and F1 score for self-verification performance[cite: 1896, 1897].

\subsection{Results}
\textbf{Reasoning Performance.} LaSeR consistently matched or outperformed the GRPO baseline across tested benchmarks. [cite_start]For instance, on the Qwen2.5-7B architecture, LaSeR achieved an average accuracy of 53.5\% compared to GRPO's 49.2\% across the five benchmarks[cite: 1885]. [cite_start]This suggests that jointly optimizing for self-verification does not degrade, and can even enhance, reasoning capabilities[cite: 1902].

\textbf{Self-Verification Capability.} The most significant result was the model's ability to verify its own answers. [cite_start]LaSeR models achieved self-verification F1 scores around 80\%, comparable to large-scale external verifiers (e.g., Qwen2.5-Math-RM-72B)[cite: 1917]. [cite_start]This capability allows for effective "inference-time scaling" via weighted majority voting, where the model's self-score is used to rank solutions[cite: 1921].

\textbf{Efficiency Verification.} The experiments confirmed that LaSeR incurs negligible cost. [cite_start]By deriving the score from the next-token probability of the final token, it requires only one additional token inference, whereas previous self-correction methods required generating entire new sequences[cite: 1728].

\section{Paper Analysis}
\label{sec:paperanalysis}

This section provides a critical analysis of the work by Yang et al. (2025) \cite{yang2025laser}, evaluating the quality of its content, methodology, and scientific presentation.

\subsection{Content Analysis}
[cite_start]\textbf{Research Motivation and Novelty.} The research question is highly relevant to the current trajectory of Large Language Model development, specifically the move towards "System 2" reasoning and test-time compute scaling[cite: 1680]. While the concept of self-verification is not new, the authors' approach to solving the *efficiency* bottleneck is novel. [cite_start]The theoretical derivation linking the verifier reward to the last-token log-probability ratio is elegant and provides a strong mathematical justification for what might otherwise be an ad-hoc engineering trick[cite: 1671].

\textbf{Methodological Soundness.} The methodology is robust. [cite_start]The authors carefully isolate variables by comparing LaSeR against a clean GRPO baseline using the same datasets and base models[cite: 1881]. [cite_start]The inclusion of a re-weighting strategy for class imbalance addresses a common pitfall in training verifiers, where negative samples often vastly outnumber positives[cite: 1833]. [cite_start]Furthermore, the empirical validation of the "constant reference probability" assumption (Figure 5 in the paper) is a strong piece of evidence that justifies their simplification for computational efficiency[cite: 1827].

\textbf{Interpretation of Results.} The interpretation that the last token's probability distribution can effectively compress the "confidence" of the entire reasoning chain is compelling. [cite_start]However, the authors note a limitation: this method performed significantly better on mathematical tasks than on general reasoning tasks like MMLU-Pro[cite: 2090]. [cite_start]The authors transparently attribute this to the inherently weaker reasoning ability of the base models in general domains and the noise in model-based verifiers used for training[cite: 2093]. This honesty strengthens the scientific integrity of the report.

\textbf{Critique of the "Last Token" Assumption.} While effective, the reliance on the last token assumes that the model aggregates all uncertainty into that final step. [cite_start]In scenarios with "reward hacking" or length bias—where incorrect solutions are often longer—the implicit reward can be unreliable[cite: 1782]. Although LaSeR mitigates length bias by using a specific token probability rather than the cumulative implicit reward, it remains to be seen if this holds for highly complex, multi-step reasoning tasks outside of mathematics where the "final token" might be less indicative of the overall process structure.

\subsection{Scientific Writing \& Layout Analysis}
\textbf{Structure and Clarity.} The paper is well-structured, following a standard logical progression. [cite_start]The distinction between the theoretical derivation (Section 3) and the practical implementation (Algorithm 1) is clear[cite: 1850]. [cite_start]The authors effectively use figures to illustrate the workflow (Figure 1), making the simultaneous training and verification process easy to grasp[cite: 1718].

[cite_start]\textbf{Reproducibility.} The paper provides detailed hyperparameters in the Appendix (e.g., learning rates, batch sizes, KL coefficients), which supports reproducibility[cite: 2291]. [cite_start]They also explicitly state the prompt templates used, which is crucial as RLVR performance is often sensitive to prompting strategies[cite: 2432].

\textbf{Visualizations.} The visualizations are informative. [cite_start]Specifically, the comparison of self-rewarding score distributions (Figure 3) clearly highlights the difference in separability between Math tasks (high separation) and General Reasoning tasks (significant overlap), providing visual evidence for the method's current limitations[cite: 2088].

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Yang et al. (2025) \cite{yang2025laser} present LaSeR, a compelling framework that addresses the efficiency gap in LLM self-verification. [cite_start]By mathematically equating the optimal verifier reward to the last-token self-rewarding score, the authors enable models to learn reasoning and verification jointly without the heavy computational tax of external verifiers or iterative self-correction[cite: 2112].

[cite_start]The study's findings reveal that this lightweight approach can match the performance of significantly larger external reward models on mathematical benchmarks[cite: 1917]. Strengths of the work include its rigorous theoretical grounding and the practical "zero-cost" nature of the resulting self-verification signal. [cite_start]However, limitations regarding generalization to non-mathematical domains and reliance on specific token probabilities suggest that further research is needed to make this a universal solution for LLM reasoning[cite: 2094]. Overall, LaSeR represents a significant step forward in making test-time scaling and self-correction computationally feasible for widespread deployment.
