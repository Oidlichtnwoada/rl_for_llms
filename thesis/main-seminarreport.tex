\submissiondepartment{Institute for Machine Learning}

\abstract{This seminar report critically reviews the work \textit{LaSeR: Reinforcement Learning with Last-Token Self-Rewarding} by \citet{laser}. The study addresses the inefficiency of current self-verification methods in Large Language Models (\textit{LLMs}) by theoretically deriving that the optimal reasoning reward can be formulated as the last-token self-rewarding score of the model. By augmenting standard Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (\textit{RLVR}) with a Mean Squared Error (\textit{MSE}) loss on the log-probability of the last token, \textit{LaSeR} jointly optimizes reasoning and verification capabilities \citep{laser}. The findings demonstrate that this approach achieves high reasoning accuracy and self-verification performance on mathematical benchmarks with negligible inference cost \citep{laser}. Strengths of the paper include its elegant theoretical foundation and significant efficiency gains. However, limitations exist regarding its generalization to non-mathematical domains and its reliance on specific prompt structures \citep{laser}. This report is organized into four sections: a story summary, a paper summary, a critical quality analysis, and an overall conclusion.}

\section{Story Summary}
\label{sec:storysummary}

\subsection*{1. What is the central question?}
The paper addresses a critical efficiency bottleneck in Large Language Models (\textit{LLMs}): How can reasoning and self-verification capabilities be unified within a single model to provide reliable signals at test time without incurring the high computational costs associated with external verifiers or sequential self-correction \citep{laser}?

\subsection*{2. Why is this question important?}
Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (\textit{RLVR}) depends on ground-truth answers. However, these are unavailable during test-time inference \citep{laser}. While verifiable answers are accessible in deterministic domains such as mathematics, they are elusive in subjective domains, such as creative writing or summarization, where assessing formal correctness remains difficult. Existing methods, such as external verifiers or self-correction prompts, suffer from inefficiency. External verifiers necessitate the training of separate models, while sequential self-verification doubles the inference cost \citep{laser}. Solving this problem enables efficient \textit{test-time scaling}, which allows models to self-select correct answers with minimal computational overhead \citep{laser}.

\subsection*{3. What evidence/data (variables) are needed to answer this question?}
To address this question, the authors must measure reasoning accuracy, denoted as \textit{Pass@1}, self-verification performance using the \textit{F1} score, and computational cost in terms of inference tokens \citep{laser}. Furthermore, they must validate the theoretical correlation between the \textit{last-token self-rewarding score}, derived from a log-probability ratio, and the true verifier reward \citep{laser}, demonstrating that this ratio can be computed via a simplified, efficient form.

\subsection*{4. What methods are used to get this evidence/data?}
\citet{laser} propose \textit{LaSeR} (Reinforcement Learning with Last-Token Self-Rewarding), an algorithm that augments the standard \textit{RLVR} objective with a Mean Squared Error (\textit{MSE}) loss. This loss aligns the model's log-probability of the last token for a specific token, such as \texttt{<vision\_start>}, with the solution's true correctness \citep{laser}. The authors evaluate this method against baselines, such as \textit{GRPO} and external Reward Models (\textit{PRMs} and \textit{ORMs}), using architectures based on \textit{LLaMA-3.2} and \textit{Qwen-2.5} \citep{laser}.

\subsection*{5. What analyses must be applied for the data to answer the central question?}
To validate the methodology, the authors must empirically demonstrate that the simplified theoretical assumption, specifically that the partition function $Z(x,y) \approx 1$, holds true \citep{laser}. Additionally, they must analyze the correlation between the learned self-rewarding scores and ground-truth correctness across various mathematical benchmarks, such as \textit{MATH500} and \textit{AIME} \citep{laser}.

\subsection*{6. What evidence/data (values for the variables) were obtained?}
\textit{LaSeR} achieved reasoning accuracy comparable to, or exceeding, standard \textit{GRPO} (e.g., $80.2\%$ vs. $79.9\%$ on \textit{MATH500} for \textit{Qwen2.5-7B}) \citep{laser}. Crucially, the method demonstrated high self-verification \textit{F1} scores of approximately $80\%$ \citep{laser}. This result matches the performance of a $72$\,B external reward model while requiring only a single additional token generation per solution \citep{laser}. Notably, these self-assessment capabilities remained robust even on difficult datasets like \textit{AIME24}, where reasoning accuracy was significantly lower, around $15\%$ \citep{laser}. This represents a substantial advancement in the ability of the model to distinguish correct from incorrect outputs at test time. This mitigation effectively addresses issues related to uncalibrated confidence.

\subsection*{7. What were the results of the analyses?}
The analysis confirmed that the \textit{last-token self-rewarding score} effectively captures solution correctness, enabling the model to act as its own verifier during both training and testing \citep{laser}. The simplification of the reference model probability to a constant was empirically validated, which further reduced computational overhead \citep{laser}. However, the method's generalization to \textit{inexact} domains, such as general reasoning tasks in \textit{MMLU-Pro}, was observed to be less effective compared to the mathematical domain \citep{laser}.

\subsection*{8. How did the analyses answer the central question?}
The results demonstrate that reasoning and verification can be jointly optimized without significant overhead \citep{laser}. By encoding the verification signal directly into the last token's probability distribution, \textit{LaSeR} eliminates the need for a secondary inference pass, effectively unifying the roles of generator and verifier \citep{laser}.

\subsection*{9. What does this answer tell us about the broader field?}
These findings imply that \textit{LLMs} possess latent self-evaluation capabilities that can be unlocked via specific RL objectives, without the need for complex architectural changes or massive external reward models \citep{laser}. This suggests a viable path toward efficient \textit{System 2} reasoning, where models inherently recognize the validity of their outputs \citep{laser}. While the concept of models quantifying uncertainty is established in Explainable AI (\textit{XAI}), this work advances the field by integrating it directly into the generation process for efficient reinforcement learning.

\subsection*{10. Did the paper answer the question satisfactorily? Why (not)?}
Yes, the paper satisfactorily answers the central question by providing a rigorous theoretical derivation supported by extensive empirical results on standard benchmarks \citep{laser}. The method's efficiency, requiring only a single additional token, directly resolves the core challenge of computational cost in self-verification \citep{laser}.

\section{Paper Summary}
\label{sec:papersummary}

This chapter summarizes the paper \textit{LaSeR: Reinforcement Learning with Last-Token Self-Rewarding} by \citet{laser}. The summary is structured to reflect the logical flow of a machine learning research paper, covering the introduction, methodology, experimental setup, and results.

\subsection{Introduction}
The landscape of Large Language Models (\textit{LLMs}) is undergoing a paradigm shift towards \textit{System 2} reasoning capabilities, where models are expected to engage in deliberate thought processes rather than simple pattern matching. Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (\textit{RLVR}) has emerged as a cornerstone for this advancement, exemplified by systems such as \textit{OpenAI o1} and \textit{DeepSeek-R1} \citep{r1}. \textit{RLVR} encourages models to produce deliberate reasoning chains by rewarding final outcomes based on strict ground-truth consistency. However, a significant operational dilemma exists: while \textit{RLVR} is effective during training where labels exist, it fails to provide verification signals during test-time inference because ground-truth answers are unavailable \citep{laser}.

To address this \textit{test-time blind spot}, prior research has relied on two primary strategies: training external verifiers (Reward Models) or utilizing sequential self-verification, such as prompting the model to \textit{double-check} its answer. \citet{laser} argue that both approaches suffer from severe inefficiency. External verifiers impose a high computational burden by requiring the maintenance and query of separate, often parameter-heavy models. Sequential self-verification unifies the model but effectively doubles the inference cost and latency by necessitating a second full generation pass to critique the first \citep{laser}. In response, the authors introduce \textit{LaSeR} (Reinforcement Learning with Last-Token Self-Rewarding), a novel algorithm designed to unify reasoning and verification within a single policy model at nearly zero additional cost \citep{laser}.

\subsection{Methods}
\textbf{Theoretical Framework.} The core contribution of the paper is the theoretical derivation linking the RL objective of verification to a closed-form solution. RL methodologies, such as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (\textit{RLHF}), utilized for model fine-tuning aim to maximize the expected reward while maintaining proximity to a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. The objective function is formally defined as:
\begin{equation}
	\max_{\pi_\theta} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_\theta(y|x)} \left[ r(x, y) - \beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta(y|x) || \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)) \right]
\end{equation}
where $r(x, y)$ denotes the reward for prompt $x$ and response $y$, and $\beta$ controls the KL-divergence penalty. This penalty is crucial because it prevents the model from deviating excessively from the base distribution. Consequently, it avoids mode collapse and ensures the retention of general language capabilities. As derived in \citet{directpreferenceopt}, the optimal policy $\pi_r$ maximizing this objective takes the closed form:
\begin{equation}
	\pi_r(y | x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y | x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
where $Z(x)$ denotes the partition function, serving as a normalization term to ensure that probabilities sum to $1$. \citet{laser} apply this relation to the prediction of a confidence token $z_c$, such as a special unused token like \texttt{<vision\_start>}, conditioned on the context $x$ and the generated reasoning $y$. Substituting the model policy $\pi_\theta(z_c | x, y)$ for $\pi_r$ and rearranging the equation to express the reward in terms of the policy yields the following reward derivation:
\begin{equation}
	r(x, y) = \beta_v \log \frac{\pi_\theta(z_c | x, y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c | x, y)} + \beta_v \log Z(x, y)
\end{equation}
\citet{laser} assume that the probability mass of $z_c$ is negligible since it is an unused special token. Text-only models often share tokenizers with larger multimodal families, such as \textit{Qwen2.5}. Thus, tokens like \texttt{<vision\_start>} have not been seen during training and maintain consistently low probability mass. Consequently, the partition function $Z(x, y)$ approximates $1$. This approximation allows for the discarding of the term $\beta_v \log Z(x, y)$. Furthermore, empirical observation suggests that the reference policy probability $\pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c | x, y)$ for such tokens is effectively constant. Applying these simplifications yields the final \textit{LaSeR} expected reward formulation:
\begin{equation}
	r_{\text{LaSeR}}(x, y) = \beta_v \log \pi_\theta(z_c | x, y) - \beta_v c_{\text{ref}}
\end{equation}
where $\beta_v$ is a scaling coefficient and $c_{\text{ref}} \approx \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c | x, y)$ represents the constant baseline. Since probabilities lie in the interval $(0, 1]$, the logarithmic term implies that this reward signal is theoretically unbounded in the negative direction \citep{laser}. This conversion transforms a global optimization problem into a local regression task.

\textbf{LaSeR Algorithm and Optimization.} Based on this theoretical insight, \citet{laser} propose a lightweight augmentation to the standard \textit{RLVR} process. \textit{LaSeR} adds a Mean Squared Error (\textit{MSE}) loss to the surrogate loss of the RL algorithm (typically scaled by a weighting factor). This auxiliary loss forces the \textit{last-token self-rewarding score} (the scaled log-probability difference) to regress towards the actual verifier reward ($0$ or $1$) derived from the ground truth. This effectively \textit{distills} the judgment of the external verifier into the probability distribution of a single token at the end of the generation \citep{laser}.

To ensure stable training, \citet{laser} introduce several crucial techniques. First, they employ a \textit{class-level loss re-weighting} strategy to handle the imbalance between correct and incorrect solutions. It prevents the model from biasing its verification towards the majority class, which is critically important for maintaining a high verification \textit{F1} score. Class-level reweighting aids differentiation, even if it does not directly improve overall reasoning accuracy. Second, they utilize Self-Rewarding-Based Advantages (\textit{SWA}). These advantages are blended into the standard RL algorithm advantages before being utilized by the RL algorithm (\textit{GRPO} in the \textit{LaSeR} paper). This creates a dense reward signal and smooths out the sparse binary feedback of the hard verifier \citep{laser}. To prevent fitting to noise, this blending is gated based on the self-rewarding score standard deviation. The advantage blending is only active if the standard deviation of the self-rewarding score is higher than a specified threshold. The advantage blending is formulated as:
\begin{equation}
	A^{\text{blended}}_i = (1 - \alpha \cdot \mathbb{I}(\sigma_{r_{\text{LaSeR}}} > \delta)) \underbrace{\frac{r_i - \mu_r}{\sigma_r + \epsilon}}_{\text{External Advantage}} + \alpha \cdot \mathbb{I}(\sigma_{r_{\text{LaSeR}}} > \delta) \underbrace{\frac{r_{\text{LaSeR}, i} - \mu_{r_{\text{LaSeR}}}}{\sigma_{r_{\text{LaSeR}}} + \epsilon}}_{\text{Internal Advantage}}
\end{equation}
where $r_{\text{LaSeR}, i}$ is the self-rewarding score of trajectory $i$, $\sigma_{r_{\text{LaSeR}}}$ represents the standard deviation of the group's self-rewarding scores, and $\delta$ denotes the threshold \citep{laser}. Additionally, the protocol includes a \textit{warmup phase} where \textit{SWA} are initially suppressed to prevent policy collapse caused by poor initial calibration \citep{laser}.

\textbf{Efficiency via Simplification.} A significant methodological innovation is the simplification of the reference model term. \citet{laser} empirically observe that the log-probability of the special token under the reference model, $\log \pi_{ref}(z_c|x,y)$, is effectively a constant $c_{ref}$ across different inputs due to the irrelevance of the token in standard pre-training. By treating this as a pre-calculated constant, \textit{LaSeR} eliminates the need for a forward pass through the reference model during the self-rewarding calculation. This reduction lowers the computational overhead to merely one additional token inference \citep{laser}.

\subsection{Experiments}
\textbf{Setup and Baselines.} The framework was evaluated using \textit{LLaMA-3.2 (3B)} and \textit{Qwen-2.5 (7B)} architectures trained on the \textit{DeepMath-103K} dataset \citep{deepmath103k}. \citet{laser} benchmarked \textit{LaSeR} against three distinct baselines: standard pre-trained base models, models trained with standard \textit{GRPO} (Group Relative Policy Optimization) without self-rewarding, and varying sizes of external Reward Models (\textit{ORMs} and \textit{PRMs}), including a $72$B parameter model \citep{laser}. It is worth noting that two variants of \textit{LaSeR} were evaluated: one variant with advantage blending (using its own self-rewarding scores for blended advantages used in the \textit{GRPO} algorithm), and another variant without advantage blending (without \textit{SWA}).

\textbf{Datasets and Metrics.} Evaluation was conducted across five rigorous mathematical reasoning benchmarks: \textit{MATH500}, \textit{AMC23}, \textit{AIME24}, \textit{AIME25}, and \textit{OlympiadBench}. The primary metrics assessed were \textit{Pass@1} accuracy for reasoning capabilities and the \textit{F1} score for self-verification performance, ensuring the model could accurately identify both correct and incorrect solutions \citep{laser}.

\subsection{Results}
\textbf{Reasoning Performance.} Contrary to the concern that multi-objective optimization might degrade performance, \textit{LaSeR} consistently matched or outperformed the \textit{GRPO} baseline. For instance, on the \textit{Qwen2.5-7B} architecture, \textit{LaSeR} with \textit{SWA} achieved an average reasoning accuracy of $42.7\%$ across all benchmarks compared to the $41.8\%$ achieved by \textit{GRPO} \citep{laser}. Without the integration of \textit{SWA}, the average reasoning accuracy across all benchmarks was $41.1\%$, which is lower than the \textit{GRPO} baseline. This indicates that the advantage blending plays a crucial role in enhancing reasoning capabilities. \citet{laser} attribute this to the improved confidence calibration and the integration of \textit{SWA} during training. This technique provides a smoother gradient signal than sparse binary rewards.

\textbf{Self-Verification and Scaling.} \textit{LaSeR} on the \textit{Qwen2.5-7B} architecture with \textit{SWA} achieved an average self-verification \textit{F1} score of $79.6\%$ across all benchmarks. The same setting without \textit{SWA} led to an even higher self-verification \textit{F1} score of $79.9\%$. This result likely stems from the more stable training achieved without the noisy blended advantages. These results are on par with the \textit{Qwen2.5-Math-RM-72B} external verifier, which achieves an average self-verification \textit{F1} score of $80.0\%$ across all benchmarks \citep{laser}. This model has ten times the parameters of the policy model. The results demonstrate that \textit{LaSeR} effectively distills the verification capabilities of a massive external reward model into the policy itself with negligible inference overhead \citep{laser}. This high-fidelity self-score enables various techniques that utilize inference compute time scaling to condense multiple answers from numerous trajectories into a single result. Such techniques include selecting the answer with the highest self-score or applying weighted majority voting, where weights are derived from the self-scores. The authors report that weighted majority voting worked particularly well. These methods effectively unlock higher performance if more inference budget is tolerable \citep{laser}.

\section{Paper Analysis}
\label{sec:paperanalysis}

This section provides a critical analysis of the work by \citet{laser}, evaluating the quality of its content, the robustness of its methodology, and the implications of its findings for the broader AI research community.

\subsection{Content Analysis}
\textbf{Theoretical Novelty and the \textit{Token Hijacking} Mechanism.} The research motivation behind \textit{LaSeR} is well-founded in the current bottleneck of \textit{LLM} deployment, specifically the high cost associated with generally verifying \textit{LLM} answers. The theoretical contribution by \citet{laser}, which equates the verifier reward to the log-probability of the last token, constitutes an elegant reduction. It transforms what is typically a complex auxiliary task, i.e., training a separate reward model, into a simple regression task on the logits of the policy model. A particularly clever aspect is the utilization of unused vocabulary tokens like \texttt{<vision\_start>}. This \textit{token hijacking} effectively repurposes the massive residual capacity of the vocabulary space of the \textit{LLM} to carry semantic metadata of correctness without interfering with the natural language generation \citep{laser}. Since the single forward pass occurs after the \texttt{EOS} token, the policy is trained to assign probability mass to that special token only after the sequence ends. Consequently, this special token does not influence regular trajectory generation. The assumption that the partition function approximates unity is more practical and valid than assuming a constant reference score. The latter, $c_{\text{ref}}$, represents a strong assumption, as the probability naturally deviates slightly. This simplification eliminates the need for a forward pass in the reference model to compute the baseline probability of the reference token \citep{laser}.

\textbf{Practical and Structural Limitations.} The \textit{LaSeR} mechanism relies on generating an additional token \textit{after} the \texttt{EOS} (End of Sequence) token. This requirement constitutes a non-standard inference mechanism. Since standard inference engines are typically engineered to terminate generation immediately upon encountering \texttt{EOS}, adopting \textit{LaSeR} necessitates modifications to low-level inference loops. This necessity may hinder \textit{plug-and-play} adoption in existing production pipelines or inference libraries and frameworks. Furthermore, by compressing all prior reasoning uncertainty into a single final step, the method may be susceptible to length bias in complex chains. In long reasoning trajectories, the probability distribution of the final token may fail to accurately reflect logic errors that occurred significantly earlier in the chain. The mechanism remains an aggregation heuristic that might fail in highly complex, multi-stage reasoning tasks where errors are subtle and distributed.

\textbf{The Generator-Verifier Gap and Calibration.} A central concept in self-verification research is the \textit{Generator-Verifier Gap}, which highlights the inherent asymmetry in complexity between the presumably harder task of generating a solution and the presumably simpler task of verifying its correctness. This gap presents a dual-edged sword: if the generative component suffers from mode collapse or consistently yields blatantly incorrect answers, the corresponding self-verification problem becomes trivial to optimize. Consequently, for self-verification targets to be meaningful, the model must possess sufficient domain capability to produce diverse and plausible reasoning trajectories. \textit{LaSeR} addresses this by mandating the prediction of a correctness score \textit{immediately} upon completion of the generation. Hypothetically, this requirement primes the model's internal representations to maintain a latent \textit{sense of correctness} throughout the generation process, thereby intrinsically benefiting reasoning capabilities. One can deduce this effect from the observation that \textit{LaSeR} achieves robust performance improvements even without the integration of Self-Rewarding-Based Advantages (\textit{SWA}), suggesting that these enhanced internal representations are a primary driver of the observed gains. However, the method remains bound by the capabilities of the ground-truth verifier used during training. In mathematical domains, this verifier is absolute. Conversely, in general reasoning, as shown by the drop in performance on \textit{MMLU-Pro}, the noise in the training verifier propagates directly into the self-rewarding score \citep{laser}. This result indicates that \textit{LaSeR} serves as an excellent amplifier of verifiable truth, yet it cannot synthesize verification capabilities \textit{ex nihilo}.

\textbf{RL Dynamics: Dense vs. Sparse Rewards.} The introduction of \textit{SWA} is a subtle but profound contribution to the Reinforcement Learning dynamics. Standard \textit{RLVR} typically employs sparse binary rewards ($0$ for incorrect, $1$ for correct). Sparse rewards complicate gradient descent optimization due to the \textit{credit assignment problem}; however, by mixing the continuous self-score of the model into the advantage function, \textit{LaSeR} effectively densifies the reward landscape \citep{laser}. This continuous signal allows the model to differentiate between \textit{confident errors} and \textit{near misses}. It likely explains the accelerated reasoning performance. With only the external verifier, RL algorithms cannot distinguish between these two failure modes, as both yield rewards of $0$. This aligns with broader findings in RL that shaping rewards leads to faster convergence. \textit{LaSeR} achieves this shaping endogenously rather than requiring manual reward engineering.

\textbf{Prompt Sensitivity.} While efficient, the method relies on specific prompt structures to trigger the verification state. The experiments show high sensitivity to prompts, a common trait in \textit{LLMs} \citep{laser}. This is because the external verifier needs to be presented with the answer in a consistent format in order to extract the answer deterministically for correctness evaluation. This answer format is provided to the model via the system prompt.

\subsection{Scientific Writing \& Layout Analysis}
\textbf{Structure and Clarity.} The paper demonstrates high-quality scientific writing. The separation between the theoretical derivation (Section 3) and the practical algorithm (Algorithm 1) aids readability. It allows readers to understand the \textit{why} before the \textit{how} \citep{laser}. The authors maintain intellectual honesty regarding their results; specifically, the comparison of self-rewarding score distributions (Figure 3) provides clear visual evidence that the self-assessment performs well in math-based, exact domains but generalizes poorly to other, more inexact domains. This visual honesty regarding the limitations of the method strengthens the credibility of the positive results \citep{laser}.

\textbf{Reproducibility.} The paper excels in terms of reproducibility. The appendix provides exhaustive details on hyperparameters (learning rates, batch sizes, $\beta$ coefficients) and, crucially, the specific prompt templates used \citep{laser}. Given that RL performance is often highly sensitive to prompting and initialization, referred to as the \textit{seed lottery}, this level of detail is commendable and necessary for the community to build upon this work \citep{laser}. Furthermore, they have provided a full GitHub repository with all their code: \url{https://github.com/RUCBM/LaSeR}.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In this work, \citet{laser} propose \textit{LaSeR}, a framework that successfully enhances the accessibility of verification capabilities for Large Language Models. By theoretically proving that the optimal reasoning reward can be compressed into the log-probability of a single token, the authors achieve a unification of \textit{actor} and \textit{critic}, which was previously computationally prohibitive \citep{laser}.

The implications of this research extend beyond simple efficiency. \textit{LaSeR} demonstrates that expensive, external $72$\,B-parameter reward models can be effectively approximated by the latent confidence scores of a $7$\,B policy model, provided the training objective is correctly aligned \citep{laser}. This advancement enables a new paradigm of \textit{inference-time scaling} in which models generate multiple solutions and self-select the optimal one with minimal latency overhead. The shift from computing implicit rewards over the entire sequence to a single-token check represents a move towards \textit{Compute-Optimal} inference, shifting the computational burden from runtime verification to training-time calibration \citep{laser}.

Future work will likely focus on closing the generalization gap observed in non-mathematical domains. Other directions may involve eliminating the dependence on the single additional forward pass after the \texttt{EOS} token to streamline integration. Future research could also explore scenarios where generated reasoning trajectories revert to a previous checkpoint if the self-rewarding score drops excessively during episode generation. This approach necessitates generating more self-reward signals, potentially at each generation step. Furthermore, there is potential to extend the current \textit{last token} verification into a finer-grained \textit{step-by-step} verification where a self-reward signal is generated at each token generation step. Overall, \textit{LaSeR} represents a decisive step towards autonomous, self-correcting AI systems that are both capably \textit{System 2} and computationally viable for widespread deployment \citep{laser}. The additional self-rewarding score further enables \textit{LLMs} to better quantify their uncertainty. This contributes to the broader goals of Explainable AI (\textit{XAI}) by providing interpretable confidence metrics alongside generated outputs.
