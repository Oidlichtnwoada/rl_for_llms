\abstract{
	We present \textbf{Dense Confidence}, a novel extension of the \textit{LaSeR} framework \citep{laser} designed to address the sparsity of reward signals in logical reasoning tasks. Unlike prior approaches that rely on a scalar confidence score at the sequence termination, our architecture compels the policy to estimate the validity of its reasoning trace at \textit{every} token step. By overloading a specialized control token (the confidence token) and applying a scaled sigmoid projection, we map latent representations to interpretable probabilities.

	We evaluate this method on a filtered subset of the \texttt{DeepMath-103K} dataset using a $0.5$B parameter policy model. While the proposed dense confidence mechanism is agnostic to the specific reinforcement learning algorithm, we employ Group Relative Policy Optimization (\textit{GRPO}) for our experiments. We define three experimental conditions, consisting of a Baseline, an Auxiliary-Loss-Only variant, and a Self-Rewarding configuration, to rigorously isolate the contribution of dense intrinsic rewards. This work provides a comprehensive analysis of the system architecture, the integration of intrinsic signals into the advantage function, and the implications for scalable oversight in resource-constrained environments. The code is available at \url{https://github.com/Oidlichtnwoada/rl_for_llms}.
}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Scalable oversight for Large Language Models (\textit{LLMs}) represents a critical frontier in AI alignment research. Conventional Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (\textit{RLHF}) \citep{rlhf} paradigms predominantly utilize outcome-based reward signals. While these signals are dense in value (continuous scalars derived from a preference model), they are sparse in time, typically provided only at the end of a trajectory. This approach is highly effective for inexact domains such as creative writing or question answering, where correctness is subjective and cannot be verified via simple deterministic rules. However, for complex, multi-step reasoning tasks in exact domains like mathematics, this formulation exacerbates the credit assignment problem, as a single scalar reward at the end of a trajectory fails to capture the validity of intermediate logical steps. In these domains, external verifiers can be used to provide exact binary rewards based on the final answer's correctness. While process supervision, rewarding granular reasoning steps via multi-step rewards, offers a remedy \citep{verifystepbystep}, it incurs a prohibitive cost in terms of high-quality human or automated annotation.

A paradigm shift is emerging in the form of \textit{Self-Rewarding Language Models}, where the resulting policy functions as its own critic. This approach aims to circumvent the \textit{alignment tax} associated with training independent reward models, facilitating scalable, self-improving systems. The \textit{LaSeR} (Last-Token Self-Rewarding) framework \citep{laser} and recent reasoning-focused reinforcement learning advancements \citep{r1} exemplify this direction by training an \textit{LLM} to predict a confidence score at the sequence terminus, utilizing this prediction as an intrinsic reward.

\subsection{Research Objectives}
We posit that a singular terminal signal is insufficient for robust process oversight in long-horizon reasoning. Consequently, we propose a \textbf{dense confidence monitoring} objective. By compelling the model to implicitly evaluate the validity of its reasoning trace at every token step, we aim to improve the calibration of uncertainty estimates.

This study implements a modified \textit{LaSeR} architecture. Our specific objectives are:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Framework Reimplementation:} To establish a robust implementation of the self-rewarding loop using modern open-weights models and specialized RL algorithms.
	\item \textbf{Dense Signal Extension:} To extend the methodology from \textit{last-token} to \textit{trajectory-wide} confidence monitoring via a custom token hook mechanism.
	\item \textbf{Ablation Analysis:} To systematically quantify the contribution of both the auxiliary confidence objective and the blended intrinsic reward through controlled experimentation.
\end{enumerate}

\autoref{sec:methods} details the technical architecture, including the mathematical formulation of the confidence function and the modified RL trainer. \autoref{sec:results} outlines the experimental results and provides an interpretation of the training dynamics. Finally, \autoref{sec:conclusion} discusses the limitations and concludes the work.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{System Architecture}
The implementation leverages the \texttt{trl} library for the training loop and \texttt{peft} for Low-Rank Adaptation (\textit{LoRA}) \citep{lora} to enable efficient training on consumer-grade hardware. Answer verification is performed using the \texttt{math-verify} package. Unlike simple string matching, this tool parses the LaTeX structure to enable semantic verification (e.g., recognizing that $\frac{1}{2}$ is equivalent to $0.5$), which is essential for robust evaluation in formal domains. It is important to note that \textit{LoRA} adapters are applied to both the attention layers and the feed-forward layers of the transformer. This distinguishes our work from the original \textit{LaSeR} implementation \citep{laser}, which relied on full-parameter fine-tuning. The core component is the \texttt{ConfidenceGRPOTrainer}, a custom subclass of \texttt{GRPOTrainer} that intercepts the forward pass to extract confidence logits.

\subsubsection{Policy Model and RL Algorithm}
We utilize a \textbf{$0.5$B parameter policy model} (Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct) to verify the concept within the significant resource constraints typical of \textit{LLM} fine-tuning, before scaling to larger architectures. This contrasts with the original \textit{LaSeR} study \citep{laser}, which utilized larger models with approximately $7$B parameters. \textbf{Reward Agnosticism:} It is important to note that the proposed dense confidence formulation is fundamentally a reward shaping mechanism. Since the confidence alignment loss is simply added to the standard RL surrogate loss, our method is robustly compatible with any reinforcement learning algorithm, such as \textit{PPO} or \textit{A2C}. However, for this implementation, we specifically select \textbf{Group Relative Policy Optimization (\textit{GRPO})} \citep{deepseekmath}.

The key advantage of this specific RL algorithm is that it eliminates the need for a separate value network (critic), which typically doubles memory requirements and is a standard component of algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (\textit{PPO}) \citep{ppo}. While other efficient fine-tuning methods like Direct Preference Optimization \citep{directpreferenceopt} also bypass the reward model by implicitly optimizing preferences, \textit{GRPO} allows us to optimize arbitrary non-differentiable reward functions directly. Instead, it estimates the baseline for the advantage function using group statistics rather than an external value network (critic), by computing the mean reward of a group of outputs $\{o_1, \dots, o_G\}$ generated from the same prompt $q$. This relative comparison stabilizes training by normalizing rewards against the model's current capability level for that specific query.

Furthermore, we set the KL-divergence coefficient ($\beta$) to a small value of $10^{-2}$. This maintains a weak regularization against diverging from the base distribution but entails the necessity of keeping a frozen reference model in memory during training. Nevertheless, a small learning rate remains crucial to prevent the model from \textit{collapsing}, losing basic grammatical and answering capabilities while optimizing for the reward signal.

\subsection{Dense Confidence Mechanism}

\subsubsection{Token Hook Mechanism}
Unlike standard generation training where only the logits of the selected tokens are relevant, our method requires the logit of the \textbf{confidence token} at \textit{every} step, regardless of what text token was actually sampled. The language model head typically projects the hidden states to the vocabulary dimension to produce these logits, which are then optionally rescaled by a temperature parameter and passed through a softmax function to yield token probabilities for sampling. For our purpose, we intercept the raw logits before sampling. Importantly, these raw logits are not rescaled by the temperature parameter either.

We selected the unused \texttt{<|vision\_pad|>} token as this confidence token. This choice is motivated by the fact that the used \textit{LLM} is a text-only model that shares the vocabulary with a multimodal model from the same model family. Consequently, the vision pad token was never seen by the chat-only model during training and remains available for overloading.

To extract these values efficiently during local training with PyTorch, we register a forward hook on the language model head:
\begin{equation}
	l_{t}^{\text{conf}} = \text{Logits}(h_t)[\texttt{id}_{\text{confidence\_token}}]
\end{equation}

where $h_t$ is the hidden state at step $t$.

\subsubsection{Scaled Sigmoid Transformation}
Raw logits are unbounded. To map them to a probability-like confidence score $c_t \in [0, 1]$, which can also be interpreted as the estimated reward of the trajectory, we employ a parameterized sigmoid function. Instead of a standard sigmoid, we use a scaling factor derived from the logistic distribution to control the slope and center of the mapping:

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:sigmoid_transform}
	c_t = \sigma\left( \frac{\pi}{\sigma_{\text{conf}}\sqrt{3}} (l_{t}^{\text{conf}} - \mu_{\text{conf}}) \right)
\end{equation}

where:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $\mu_{\text{conf}}$: Centers the effective range of the logits (set to the empirically estimated mean of confidence logits).
	\item $\sigma_{\text{conf}}$: Controls the \textit{temperature} or sensitivity of the confidence mapping (set to the empirically estimated standard deviation of confidence logits).
\end{itemize}
The scaling factor $\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}}$ is derived from the statistical property that a logistic distribution with scale parameter $s$ has a variance of $\frac{s^2 \pi^2}{3}$. By setting the scaling coefficient to this value, we align the standard deviation of the input logits (assuming they are normally distributed) with the coherent probability space of the sigmoid function, ensuring that the gradients are well-behaved during backpropagation. Furthermore, this scaling ensures that the effective output range of the logits maps comprehensively to the probability interval $[0, 1]$. Consequently, the model is only required to concentrate its output mass within a suitable sub-range corresponding to its estimated capability. We experimentally verified that the confidence logits are indeed approximately normally distributed, justifying this specific transformation.

\subsection{Training Algorithm: RL with Confidence}
The core objective of the \textit{LaSeR} framework is to enable the model to self-evaluate, thereby providing a denser reward signal than the sparse binary feedback typically available in mathematical reasoning tasks. This section delineates the theoretical formulation of the expected reward in the original \textit{LaSeR} proposal and contrasts it with the bounded dense implementation employed in this work.

\subsubsection{Theoretical Formulation: The \textit{LaSeR} Objective}
Standard Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (\textit{RLHF}) aims to maximize the expected reward while maintaining proximity to a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ to prevent mode collapse. The objective function is formally defined as:

\begin{equation}
	\max_{\pi_\theta} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_\theta(y|x)} \left[ r(x, y) - \beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta(y|x) || \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)) \right]
\end{equation}

where $r(x, y)$ is the reward for prompt $x$ and response $y$, and $\beta$ controls the KL-divergence penalty. As derived in \citet{directpreferenceopt}, the optimal policy $\pi_r$ allowing to maximize this objective takes the closed form:

\begin{equation}
	\pi_r(y | x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y | x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}

where $Z(x)$ is the partition function. Rearranging this equation allows us to express the reward function in terms of the optimal policy and the reference policy. The \textit{LaSeR} framework \citep{laser} applies this relation to the prediction of a confidence token $z_c$ conditioned on the context $x$ and the generated reasoning $y$. Substituting the model's policy $\pi_\theta(z_c | x, y)$ for $\pi_r$, the reward derivation becomes:

\begin{equation}
	r(x, y) = \beta_v \log \frac{\pi_\theta(z_c | x, y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c | x, y)} + \beta_v \log Z(x, y)
\end{equation}

\citet{laser} assume that, because $z_c$ is an unused special token, its probability mass is negligible. Therefore, the partition function $Z(x, y)$ approximates to $1$, allowing the term $\beta_v \log Z(x, y)$ to be discarded. Furthermore, empirical observation suggests that the reference policy probability $\pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c | x, y)$ for such tokens is effectively constant.

Applying these simplifications yields the final \textit{LaSeR} expected reward formulation:

\begin{equation}
	r_{\text{laser}}(x, y) = \beta_v \log \pi_\theta(z_c | x, y) - \beta_v c_{\text{ref}}
\end{equation}

where $\beta_v$ is a scaling coefficient and $c_{\text{ref}} \approx \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c | x, y)$ is the constant baseline. Since probabilities lie in $(0, 1]$, the logarithmic term implies that this reward signal is theoretically unbounded in the negative direction, contrasting with our dense sigmoid approach.

\subsubsection{Our Implementation: Bounded Dense Confidence}
In contrast to the log-probability formulation of \textit{LaSeR}, our implementation adopts a bounded, logit-based approach for the intrinsic reward calculation. We define the reward $r_{\text{dense}}$ as the average of the sigmoid-transformed logits of the confidence token computed at every step of the reasoning trace:

\begin{equation}
	r_{\text{dense}} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \sigma\left( \frac{\pi}{\sigma_{\text{conf}}\sqrt{3}} (l_{t}^{\text{conf}} - \mu_{\text{conf}}) \right)
\end{equation}

where $l_{t}^{\text{conf}}$ is the raw logit of the special confidence token at step $t$, and parameters $\mu_{\text{conf}}$ and $\sigma_{\text{conf}}$ calibrate the mapping as described in Eq. (\ref{eq:sigmoid_transform}).

\subsubsection{Comparative Analysis}
The divergence between the two formulations entails significant implications for training stability and interpretation:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Value Range and Boundedness:} The \textit{LaSeR} formulation $r_{\text{laser}}$ depends on $\log \pi_\theta$. As $\pi_\theta \to 0$, $r_{\text{laser}} \to -\infty$. This unboundedness can introduce high variance in the advantage estimates $A_i$, potentially destabilizing the policy gradient updates, especially early in training when the model is poorly calibrated. Our formulation maps confidence to a strictly bounded interval $[0, 1]$ via the sigmoid function, ensuring that the intrinsic reward signal remains within a controlled magnitude, consistent with the scale of the external binary rewards ($0$ or $1$).

	\item \textbf{Granularity:} The \textit{LaSeR} implementation provides a sparse intrinsic signal, computing the self-reward via an additional forward pass executed after the generation concludes to predict the confidence token at the sequence terminus. In contrast, our dense approach evaluates the confidence token at every generation step $t$ during the initial rollout, aggregating a trajectory-wide belief. This aims to provide a more holistic assessment of the reasoning chain rather than just a post-hoc validation of the final state.

	\item \textbf{Optimization Landscape:} Maximizing the log-probability (\textit{LaSeR}) is consistent with maximum likelihood estimation, pushing the model to eagerly assign mass to the confidence token. Maximizing the sigmoid of the logit (our method) is a softer objective that saturates as the logit increases, potentially preventing overconfidence where the model pushes logits to infinity to satisfy a logarithmic objective.

	\item \textbf{Interference Avoidance via Gradient Saturation:} A critical structural constraint of our dense monitoring approach is that the designated confidence token must strictly remain latent. In the original \textit{LaSeR} formulation, high confidence logits are unproblematic because the confidence estimation occurs only after the generation is complete (either as an appended token or a separate forward pass), preventing any interference with the reasoning trajectory. In contrast, since we evaluate this token at every step $t$, if its logit $l_t^{\text{conf}}$ were to increase sufficiently to rival the top-$k$ tokens, it would be sampled during generation, corrupting the logical reasoning trace. Our scaled sigmoid formulation inherently prevents this pathology. Unlike the logarithmic objective which implies a constant gradient drive towards maximizing probability (pushing logits towards $+\infty$), the sigmoid function saturates rapidly once $l_t^{\text{conf}}$ marginally exceeds the effective range defined by $\mu_{\text{conf}}$ and $\sigma_{\text{conf}}$. Upon saturation ($c_t \approx 1$), the gradient vanishes, effectively arresting the upward drift of the logit at a value that remains numerically subordinate to the active vocabulary, thereby guaranteeing that the self-monitoring process remains non-intrusive.
\end{enumerate}

Despite these differences in reward calculation, we adhere to the \textit{LaSeR} protocol of blending this intrinsic signal into the advantage function of the \textit{GRPO} algorithm.

The objective function maximizes the following surrogate:

\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{J}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{q \sim P} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^G \left( \frac{1}{|o_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|} \min \left( \rho_{i,t} A_i, \text{clip}(\rho_{i,t}, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon) A_i \right) - \beta_{\text{KL}} D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta || \pi_{\text{ref}}) \right) \right]
\end{equation}

where $\rho_{i,t} = \frac{\pi_\theta(o_{i,t} | q, o_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\text{old}}(o_{i,t} | q, o_{i,<t})}$ is the importance sampling ratio, and $A_i$ is the advantage tailored to the specific variant. Standard \textit{GRPO} computes this advantage as:
\begin{equation}
	A_i = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(\{r_1 \dots r_G\})}{\text{std}(\{r_1 \dots r_G\}) + \epsilon}
\end{equation}
In our configuration, $\beta_{\text{KL}} = 10^{-2}$, providing weak regularization while relying additionally on the clipping mechanism and learning rate for stability.

\subsubsection{Auxiliary Confidence Loss}
The model is trained to minimize a joint loss: $L_{\text{total}} = - \mathcal{J}(\theta) + \beta_{\text{conf}} L_{\text{conf}}$, where the auxiliary coefficient $\beta_{\text{conf}}$ controls the magnitude of the confidence objective.
The confidence loss $L_{\text{conf}}$ acts as a regularizer, forcing the confidence scores to predict the eventual correctness of the answer. However, the coefficient $\beta_{\text{conf}}$ must not be set too high, to avoid destroying the pre-trained reasoning policy of the base model. We use Binary Cross Entropy (\textit{BCE}) between the dense confidence estimates and the broadcasted final reward $r$:

\begin{equation}
	L_{\text{conf}} = \text{BCE}(\mathbf{c}, \mathbf{r}) = - \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} [r \log(c_t) + (1-r) \log(1-c_t)]
\end{equation}

To prevent mode collapse and ensure robust self-verification performance on both ground-truth correct and incorrect trajectories, we apply dynamic importance sampling weights calculated per-group to mitigate the effects of class imbalance. In complex reasoning tasks, the policy initially generates predominantly incorrect solutions. Consequently, a standard unweighted loss would bias the confidence head towards trivially predicting failure ($c_t \approx 0$), as it would learn the prior probability of success rather than discriminating based on the reasoning trace. To counteract this, we compute dynamic weights $w_{\text{pos}}$ and $w_{\text{neg}}$ for each group of $G$ generations based on the counts of correct ($N_{\text{pos}}$) and incorrect ($N_{\text{neg}}$) trajectories:

\begin{equation}
	w_{\text{pos}} = \frac{G}{2 N_{\text{pos}}}, \quad w_{\text{neg}} = \frac{G}{2 N_{\text{neg}}}
\end{equation}

(where the weight is set to $0$ if the corresponding count is $0$). These weights ensure that the total contribution of positive and negative examples to the loss is balanced within each update step. The reweighted auxiliary loss for a specific group is defined as the mean of the reweighted \textit{BCE} losses for each trajectory:

\begin{equation}
	L_{\text{conf}}^{\text{group}} = \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} w_{r_i} \left( - \frac{1}{T_i} \sum_{t=1}^{T_i} [r_i \log(c_{i,t}) + (1-r_i) \log(1-c_{i,t})] \right)
\end{equation}

where $r_i \in \{0, 1\}$ is the ground truth binary reward for trajectory $i$, and $w_{r_i}$ is the class weight corresponding to the label $r_i$. This scheme effectively simulates a balanced dataset, forcing the model to learn features indicative of correctness rather than exploiting base rate imbalances.

\subsubsection{Self-Reward Integration (Advantage Blending)}
A key innovation is blending the external sparse reward with the internal dense confidence \citep{laser}. Standard RL struggles when the reward signal is sparse, taking \textbf{only} values of $0$ or $1$ as is common with mathematical verifiers. In this setting, the agent either stumbles upon a solution by chance for difficult questions or learns nothing. A dense reward helps the algorithm differentiate between \textit{completely wrong} and \textit{almost correct} trajectories, whereas both would receive a $0$ reward from the external verifier. This dense signal facilitates iterative improvements, particularly for questions at the frontier of the model's capabilities, provided the self-verification mechanism is sufficiently accurate.

In the \textit{Self-Rewarding} variant, we blend the standard advantage with a standardized confidence score. The blended advantage $A^{\text{blended}}_i$ for trajectory $i$ is defined as:

\begin{equation}
	A^{\text{blended}}_i =
	\begin{cases}
		(1 - \alpha) \displaystyle\frac{r_i - \mu_r}{\sigma_r + \epsilon} + \alpha \displaystyle\frac{\bar{c}_i - \mu_{\bar{c}}}{\sigma_{\bar{c}} + \epsilon} & \text{if } \sigma_{\bar{c}} > \delta \\[12pt]
		\displaystyle\frac{r_i - \mu_r}{\sigma_r + \epsilon}                                                                                                  & \text{otherwise}
	\end{cases}
\end{equation}

where $\bar{c}_i = \frac{1}{T_i} \sum_{t=1}^{T_i} c_{i,t}$ is the mean confidence of trajectory $i$, $\alpha=0.1$ is the blending factor, $\mu_r$ and $\sigma_r$ denote the mean and standard deviation of ground-truth rewards within the group, $\mu_{\bar{c}}$ and $\sigma_{\bar{c}}$ denote the mean and standard deviation of mean confidences within the group, and the threshold $\delta = 0.1$ gates the intrinsic reward contribution. This formulation ensures that intrinsic rewards only influence the policy when the model exhibits sufficient discriminative variance in its confidence estimates across the group. If the model is equally uncertain about all samples in the group (i.e., $\sigma_{\bar{c}} \leq \delta$), we default to the external reward to prevent the RL algorithm from fitting to noise.

Crucially, as established in the original \textit{LaSeR} protocol \citep{laser}, the validity of this intrinsic signal depends on the model's ability to accurately estimate confidence. To prevent the policy from optimizing towards maximizing a random or poorly calibrated signal early in training, we implement a \textit{warmup phase}. During these initial steps, the blending factor is effectively forced to $\alpha=0$, meaning the policy updates rely exclusively on the sparse ground-truth rewards from the external verifier. The intrinsic confidence signal is only integrated into the advantage function once the auxiliary loss indicates that the internal confidence estimation has stabilized. This is implemented using a configurable warmup period of several optimizer steps that must be executed before activating the blended advantages.

\subsection{Dataset and Preprocessing}
We utilize the \texttt{Keven16/LaSeR\_training\_data} (specifically the \texttt{DeepMath-103K} subset \citep{deepmath103k}). The dataset consists of math problems requiring step-by-step reasoning. In contrast to the original \textit{LaSeR} study \citep{laser} which utilized the full dataset, we apply specific constraints to accommodate local hardware limitations:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Filtering:} Rows are filtered based on the limited amount of completion tokens to ensure the generation fits within the compute budget. These filters are applied identically to both the training and evaluation sets.
	\item \textbf{Format:} Prompts are formatted using the chat template for the specific \textit{LLM} (\textit{user} and \textit{system} roles). The system message is particularly important to instruct the model to provide the answer in a format that is processable by the external verifier using deterministic functions. Specifically, the model is directed to enclose the final answer in a \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}} command at the end of the output. This last boxed expression is extracted as the prediction for the \texttt{math-verify} library, thus avoiding the need for a stochastic reward model. The maximum length for the prompt (including system message) is restricted to $128$ tokens, and the maximum generation length is limited to $1024$ tokens.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Experimental Variants}
To ensure scientific rigor, we define four controlled settings:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{BASE (base):} The untouched base model without unweighted finetuning, serving as a reference point.
	\item \textbf{GRPO (-l-r):} Standard RL training. No confidence loss, no confidence reward.
	\item \textbf{ONLY\_CONFLOSS (+l-r):} RL + Auxiliary Confidence Loss. The model is optimized for the RL loss and the confidence loss, but the self-predicted confidence is not used in the advantages used in the RL algorithm.
	\item \textbf{WITH\_CONFREW (+l+r):} RL + Auxiliary Confidence Loss + Reward Integration. This variant uses the self-proposed rewards blended into the advantages of the RL algorithm.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hardware and Compute}
Training was performed on a local workstation. Each run utilized a custom optimized generation loop in PyTorch to maximize throughput. Total training time per variant was restricted to a single epoch. This deviates from the original \textit{LaSeR} protocol \citep{laser}, which trained for two epochs. We restricted training to one epoch to align with the reduced dataset size and prevent overfitting on the proxy model.

\subsection{Training Dynamics}

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\subsection{Technical Limitations}
A primary constraint of this study is the computational intensity inherent to \textit{LLM} fine-tuning. We implemented the training loop in pure PyTorch rather than using high-throughput libraries like \texttt{vllm} for the entire pipeline, as the latter currently lack full support for Metal Performance Shaders (\textit{MPS}) required for local debugging. However, for efficient data collection, we could utilize the specific interface \texttt{LogitsProcessor} from the \texttt{vllm} library to modify the logits during the rollout generation. While this accelerates the generation phase, \texttt{vllm} supports only this initial inference step. No gradient flow is possible through the engine. Consequently, the training pass necessitates a separate PyTorch forward pass to capture gradients for the confidence auxiliary loss, introducing complexity in synchronizing state between the inference engine and the training loop. Finally, the reliance on a $0.5$B parameter proxy model bounds the upper limit of reasoning complexity, potentially masking emergent behaviors observable only at larger scales.

\subsection{Conclusion}
This study validates the feasibility of \textbf{Dense Confidence} as a mechanism for self-supervised process oversight. We demonstrate that standard \textit{LLMs} can be fine-tuned to function as concurrent verifiers without significant architectural modifications. The experimental results highlight that while auxiliary objectives improve representation learning, the active integration of self-rewards requires careful balancing to outperform strong baselines. Future research should investigate training explicit Process Reward Models (\textit{PRM}) \citep{verifystepbystep} via this self-supervised signal, potentially eliminating the dependency on costly human-labeled logical traces.
