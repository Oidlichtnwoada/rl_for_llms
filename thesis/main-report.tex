\abstract{
	We present \textbf{Dense Confidence}, a novel extension of the \textit{LaSeR} framework \citep{laser} designed to address the sparsity of reward signals in logical reasoning tasks. Unlike prior approaches that rely on a scalar confidence score at the sequence termination, our architecture compels the policy to estimate the validity of its reasoning trace at \textit{every} token step. By overloading a specialized control token (the confidence token) and applying a scaled sigmoid projection on its raw logit---rather than its probability---we map latent representations to interpretable confidence scores while provably avoiding interference with the sampling distribution.

	We evaluate this method on a filtered subset of the \texttt{\textit{GSM8K}} dataset \citep{gsm8k} using a $0.5$B parameter policy model. While the proposed dense confidence mechanism is agnostic to the specific reinforcement learning algorithm, we employ Group Relative Policy Optimization (\textit{GRPO}) \citep{deepseekmath} for our experiments. We define three experimental conditions---a pure \textit{GRPO} baseline, an Auxiliary-Loss-Only variant, and a Self-Rewarding configuration---alongside an untrained base model reference. All trained variants demonstrate a consistent and substantial improvement in answer accuracy (\textit{Pass@1} rising from approximately $31\%$ to $53\%$), while the confidence-trained variants additionally acquire a strong binary classification capability (Matthews Correlation Coefficient up to $0.58$) with zero observed instances of the confidence token being sampled during generation. The code is available at \url{https://github.com/Oidlichtnwoada/rl_for_llms}.
}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Scalable oversight for Large Language Models (\textit{LLMs}) represents a critical frontier in AI alignment research. Conventional Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (\textit{RLHF}) \citep{rlhf} paradigms predominantly utilize outcome-based reward signals. While these signals are dense in value (continuous scalars derived from a preference model), they are sparse in time, typically provided only at the end of a trajectory. This approach is highly effective for inexact domains such as creative writing or question answering, where correctness is subjective and cannot be verified via simple deterministic rules. However, for complex, multi-step reasoning tasks in exact domains like mathematics, this formulation exacerbates the credit assignment problem, as a single scalar reward at the end of a trajectory fails to capture the validity of intermediate logical steps. In these domains, external verifiers can be used to provide exact binary rewards based on the final answer's correctness. While process supervision---rewarding granular reasoning steps via multi-step rewards---offers a remedy \citep{verifystepbystep}, it incurs a prohibitive cost in terms of high-quality human or automated annotation.

A paradigm shift is emerging in the form of \textit{Self-Rewarding Language Models}, where the resulting policy functions as its own critic. This approach aims to circumvent the \textit{alignment tax} associated with training independent reward models, facilitating scalable, self-improving systems. The \textit{LaSeR} (Last-Token Self-Rewarding) framework \citep{laser} and recent reasoning-focused reinforcement learning advancements \citep{r1} exemplify this direction by training an \textit{LLM} to predict a confidence score at the sequence terminus, utilizing this prediction as an intrinsic reward.

\subsection{Research Objectives}
We posit that a singular terminal signal is insufficient for robust process oversight in long-horizon reasoning. Consequently, we propose a \textbf{dense confidence monitoring} objective. By compelling the model to implicitly evaluate the validity of its reasoning trace at every token step, we aim to improve the calibration of uncertainty estimates.

This study implements a modified \textit{LaSeR} architecture. Our specific objectives are:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Framework Reimplementation:} To establish a robust implementation of the self-rewarding loop using modern open-weights models, the latest library versions available at the time of execution, and specialized reinforcement learning algorithms.
	\item \textbf{Dense Signal Extension:} To extend the methodology from \textit{last-token} to \textit{trajectory-wide} confidence monitoring via a custom token hook mechanism, formulated on raw logits rather than probabilities to avoid interference with the sampling distribution.
	\item \textbf{Ablation Analysis:} To systematically quantify the contribution of both the auxiliary confidence objective and the blended intrinsic reward through controlled experimentation on the \texttt{\textit{GSM8K}} dataset \citep{gsm8k}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Structure of the Report}
\autoref{sec:methods} details the technical architecture, including the mathematical formulation of the confidence function, the modified RL trainer, the dataset, and the hardware setup. \autoref{sec:results} presents the experimental results with a quantitative interpretation of the answer accuracy and confidence prediction metrics. Finally, \autoref{sec:conclusion} discusses the limitations, scaling potential, and conclusions.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{System Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}
The implementation leverages the \texttt{trl} library for the \textit{GRPO} training loop and \texttt{peft} for Low-Rank Adaptation (\textit{LoRA}) \citep{lora} to enable efficient training on a single GPU. Answer verification is performed using the \texttt{math-verify} package. Unlike simple string matching, this tool parses the LaTeX structure to enable semantic verification (e.g., recognizing that $\frac{1}{2}$ is equivalent to $0.5$), which is essential for robust evaluation in formal domains.

The core component is the \texttt{Confidence\textit{GRPO}Trainer}, a custom subclass of \texttt{\textit{GRPO}Trainer} that intercepts the model's forward pass via a PyTorch forward hook on the language model head to extract confidence logits at every generation step. Two reward functions are employed: (1)~a \textbf{correctness reward} that applies semantic math verification against the ground-truth answer, returning $1$ for a verified match and $0$ otherwise; and (2)~a \textbf{format reward} that returns $1$ if the completion contains at least one \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}} expression and $0$ otherwise, ensuring the model consistently produces answers in a verifiable format.

\subsubsection{Policy Model and RL Algorithm}
We utilize a $0.5$B parameter policy model (\texttt{\textit{Qwen}/\textit{Qwen}2.5-0.5B-Instruct}) to verify the concept within significant resource constraints, before scaling to larger architectures. This contrasts with the original \textit{LaSeR} study \citep{laser}, which utilized larger models with approximately $7$B parameters.

\textbf{Reward Agnosticism.} The proposed dense confidence formulation is fundamentally a reward shaping mechanism. Since the confidence alignment loss is simply added to the standard RL surrogate loss, our method is robustly compatible with any reinforcement learning algorithm, including \textit{PPO} \citep{ppo} or \textit{A2C}. For this implementation, we select \textbf{Group Relative Policy Optimization (\textit{GRPO})} \citep{deepseekmath}. The key advantage of \textit{GRPO} is that it eliminates the need for a separate value network (critic), which typically doubles memory requirements and is a standard component of \textit{PPO}. While other efficient fine-tuning methods like Direct Preference Optimization \citep{directpreferenceopt} bypass the reward model by implicitly optimizing preferences, \textit{GRPO} allows us to optimize arbitrary non-differentiable reward functions directly. It estimates the advantage baseline using group statistics---computing the mean reward of a group of $G$ outputs $\{o_1, \dots, o_G\}$ generated from the same prompt~$q$---rather than relying on an external value network.

In our configuration, the KL-divergence coefficient $\beta_{\mathrm{KL}}$ is set to zero. This eliminates the KL penalty term entirely, meaning no frozen reference model is needed for regularization during training, which saves another additional inference pass. As a consequence, training stability relies exclusively on the clipping mechanism of the surrogate objective and a small learning rate $\eta$. While a nonzero $\beta_{\mathrm{KL}}$ would provide additional regularization against policy drift, setting it to zero may make the training more unstable as the policy could be altered too far from the initial policy. We found that the combination of clipping and a conservative learning rate sufficed for stable training on the \texttt{\textit{GSM8K}} domain. We note that setting $\beta_{\mathrm{KL}} = 0$ amplifies the importance of properly tuning the confidence loss factor $\beta_{\mathrm{conf}}$, as discussed in \autoref{sec:conf_loss_sensitivity}.

\subsection{Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning with \textit{LoRA}}
\label{sec:lora}
We employ Low-Rank Adaptation (\textit{LoRA}) \citep{lora} to reduce the number of trainable parameters. \textit{LoRA} decomposes the weight update $\Delta W$ of a pre-trained weight matrix $W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$ into two low-rank matrices $A \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r_{\mathrm{LoRA}}}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{r_{\mathrm{LoRA}} \times k}$, such that the adapted forward pass computes $h = W_0 x + \frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{LoRA}}}{r_{\mathrm{LoRA}}} B A x$, where $r_{\mathrm{LoRA}} \ll \min(d,k)$ is the rank and $\alpha_{\mathrm{LoRA}}$ is a scaling factor. This reduces the trainable parameters from $d \times k$ to $(d + k) \times r_{\mathrm{LoRA}}$ per adapted layer.

\textit{LoRA} adapters are applied to \textbf{all linear layers} of the transformer (both attention and feed-forward modules), yielding exactly $8{,}799{,}104$ trainable parameters out of $502{,}831{,}872$ total parameters, corresponding to $1.7499\%$ of the model. This distinguishes our work from the original \textit{LaSeR} implementation \citep{laser}, which relied on full-parameter fine-tuning.

\subsubsection{Confidence Token Embedding Training}
\label{sec:embedding_training}
A critical design decision is the explicit full-finetuning of the confidence token's embedding vector, enabled via the \texttt{trainable\_token\_indices} mechanism of the \textit{LoRA} configuration. In weight-tied transformer architectures---such as the one employed here---the token embedding matrix and the output projection layer (the linear layer at the end) share identical weight parameters. Consequently, updating the embedding vector of the confidence token directly modifies the computation of its output logit, providing the single biggest lever for aligning the otherwise untrained token's logit to the desired confidence range. This is a good idea because it allows the model to rapidly adjust the baseline confidence logit without needing to route complex gradients through the entire transformer stack.

Without this mechanism, the model would be forced to adjust all intermediate hidden representations through the low-rank adapter updates alone, in order to compensate for an output projection that produces essentially random logits for a token that was never seen during pre-training. Direct embedding training provides a dramatically more efficient optimization path: the output layer can rapidly adapt to the shifted sigmoid center (see \autoref{sec:interference}), making the confidence mapping both faster to learn and more robust to initialize.

\subsection{Dense Confidence Mechanism}

\subsubsection{Token Hook Mechanism}
Unlike standard generation training where only the logits of the selected tokens are relevant, our method requires the logit of the \textbf{confidence token} at \textit{every} step, regardless of what text token was actually sampled. The language model head projects the hidden states to the vocabulary dimension to produce logits, which are then optionally rescaled by a temperature parameter and passed through a softmax function to yield token probabilities for sampling. For our purpose, we intercept the raw logits before any rescaling or sampling.

We selected the unused \texttt{<|vision\_pad|>} token as the confidence token. This choice is motivated by the fact that the employed \textit{LLM} is a text-only model sharing the vocabulary with a multimodal sibling from the same model family. Consequently, the vision pad token was never seen by the text-only model during pre-training and remains available for overloading.

To extract these values efficiently during local training with PyTorch, we register a forward hook on the language model head:
\begin{equation}
	l_{t}^{\mathrm{conf}} = \mathrm{Logits}(h_t)[\texttt{id}_{\mathrm{conf}}]
\end{equation}
where $h_t$ is the hidden state at step $t$ and $\texttt{id}_{\mathrm{conf}}$ is the vocabulary index of the confidence token.

\subsubsection{Scaled Sigmoid Transformation}
Raw logits are unbounded. To map them to a confidence score $c_t \in [0, 1]$, interpretable as the estimated reward of the trajectory, we employ a parameterized sigmoid function. Instead of a standard sigmoid, we use a scaling factor derived from the logistic distribution:
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:sigmoid_transform}
	c_t = \sigma\!\left( \frac{\pi}{\sigma_{\mathrm{conf}}\sqrt{3}} \left(l_{t}^{\mathrm{conf}} - \mu_{\mathrm{conf}}\right) \right)
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $\mu_{\mathrm{conf}}$: The centering parameter, set to the empirically estimated mean of the confidence token logit across the training corpus.
	\item $\sigma_{\mathrm{conf}}$: The scaling parameter, set to the empirically estimated standard deviation of the confidence token logit.
\end{itemize}
The factor $\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}}$ aligns the standard deviation of a logistic distribution with that of a Gaussian: a logistic with scale $s$ has variance $\frac{s^2 \pi^2}{3}$. By setting the scaling coefficient accordingly, we ensure that the effective output range of normally distributed logits maps comprehensively to $[0, 1]$, such that the model need only concentrate its output within the natural sub-range corresponding to its calibrated capability. We experimentally verified that the confidence logits are indeed approximately normally distributed.

\subsection{Training Algorithm: RL with Confidence}
\label{sec:training_algorithm}
The core objective of the \textit{LaSeR} framework is to enable the model to self-evaluate, thereby providing a denser reward signal than the sparse binary feedback typically available in mathematical reasoning tasks.

\subsubsection{Theoretical Formulation: The \textit{LaSeR} Objective}
Standard \textit{RLHF} aims to maximize the expected reward while maintaining proximity to a reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$:
\begin{equation}
	\max_{\pi_\theta} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, y \sim \pi_\theta(y|x)} \left[ r(x, y) - \beta\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_\theta(y|x) \| \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y|x)\right) \right]
\end{equation}
As derived in \citet{directpreferenceopt}, the optimal policy takes the closed form $\pi_r(y|x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y|x)\exp\!\bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr)$. The \textit{LaSeR} framework \citep{laser} applies this relation to the prediction of a confidence token $z_c$ conditioned on context $x$ and generated reasoning $y$, obtaining after simplification:
\begin{equation}
	r_{\mathrm{laser}}(x, y) = \beta_v \log \pi_\theta(z_c \mid x, y) - \beta_v\, c_{\mathrm{ref}}
\end{equation}
where $\beta_v$ is a scaling coefficient and $c_{\mathrm{ref}} \approx \log \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(z_c \mid x,y)$ is treated as a constant. Since probabilities lie in $(0,1]$, the logarithmic term is theoretically unbounded in the negative direction.

\subsubsection{Our Implementation: Bounded Dense Confidence}
In contrast to the log-probability formulation of \textit{LaSeR}, our implementation adopts a bounded, \textbf{logit-based} approach. We define the intrinsic reward $r_{\mathrm{dense}}$ as the mean of the sigmoid-transformed confidence logits across the entire reasoning trace:
\begin{equation}
	r_{\mathrm{dense}} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \sigma\!\left( \frac{\pi}{\sigma_{\mathrm{conf}}\sqrt{3}} \left(l_{t}^{\mathrm{conf}} - \mu_{\mathrm{conf}}\right) \right)
\end{equation}
This formulation operates on raw logits \textit{before} the softmax normalization, in contrast to \textit{LaSeR} which operates on the \textit{probability} after softmax. This distinction is critical for training stability, as elaborated below.

\subsubsection{Comparative Analysis}
\label{sec:interference}
The divergence between the two formulations entails significant implications:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Value Range and Boundedness.} The \textit{LaSeR} formulation depends on $\log \pi_\theta$, which diverges as $\pi_\theta \to 0$. This unboundedness can introduce high variance in advantage estimates, destabilizing policy gradient updates. Our sigmoid formulation maps confidence to $[0,1]$, consistent with the scale of the external binary rewards ($0$ or $1$).

	\item \textbf{Granularity.} The \textit{LaSeR} implementation provides a sparse intrinsic signal via a separate forward pass at the sequence terminus. Our dense approach evaluates the confidence token at every generation step $t$, providing a trajectory-wide assessment of the reasoning chain.

	\item \textbf{Gradient Properties and Sampling Interference.} In the \textit{LaSeR} formulation, maximizing $\log\pi_\theta(z_c|x,y)$ is equivalent to maximizing the softmax probability of the confidence token. This objective provides a constant gradient drive toward increasing the confidence logit without saturation, because $\frac{\partial}{\partial l^{\mathrm{conf}}} \log \pi_\theta(z_c) = 1 - \pi_\theta(z_c)$, which remains positive until $\pi_\theta(z_c) = 1$. At sufficient logit magnitude, the confidence token dominates the softmax distribution and is sampled during generation, corrupting the reasoning trace. This interference was identified as a principal challenge in the original \textit{LaSeR} paper \citep{laser}.

	      Our sigmoid formulation operates on the raw logit \textit{before} softmax computation. The sigmoid saturates rapidly once $l_t^{\mathrm{conf}}$ marginally exceeds the effective range defined by $\mu_{\mathrm{conf}}$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{conf}}$: upon saturation ($c_t \approx 1$), the gradient vanishes, arresting upward drift of the logit at a value that remains numerically subordinate to the active vocabulary. The initial logit of the untrained confidence token is empirically centered at $\mu_{\mathrm{conf}}$, which is so small relative to the vocabulary tokens that it is irrelevant for sampling. We confirmed across all $N_{\mathrm{eval}}$ evaluation samples that the confidence token was \textbf{never sampled} (inclusion rate: $0.0\%$ for every variant), thereby rendering the non-standard additional forward pass of \textit{LaSeR} obsolete and providing a mechanism to output the confidence score at every step without disturbing the sampling distribution.

	\item \textbf{Permanent Resolution via Embedding Training.} Even in the hypothetical case of upward logit drift, the combination of full-finetuned confidence token embedding (\autoref{sec:embedding_training}) and the parameterized sigmoid provides an additional safety mechanism. The centering parameter $\mu_{\mathrm{conf}}$ can be shifted further in the negative direction, and because the output projection layer shares its weights with the embedding layer, the model rapidly adapts to the new center. This renders the problem of confidence-sampling interference \textit{permanently} resolved, in contrast to the \textit{LaSeR} approach where the unbounded log-probability objective provides no analogous safety valve.

	\item \textbf{Binary Classification vs.\ Regression.} Our formulation naturally frames the confidence estimation as a binary classification task (via the sigmoid and \textit{BCE} loss), which is conceptually cleaner than the continuous regression implicit in maximizing a log-probability. The binary framing enables the use of well-established classification metrics (\textit{MCC}, F1, ROC-AUC) for evaluation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{\textit{GRPO} Objective}
Despite these differences in reward calculation, we adhere to the \textit{LaSeR} protocol of blending the intrinsic signal into the advantage function. The \textit{GRPO} objective maximizes:
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{J}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{q \sim P} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^G \left( \frac{1}{|o_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|} \min\!\bigl( \rho_{i,t} A_i,\; \mathrm{clip}(\rho_{i,t}, 1{-}\epsilon, 1{+}\epsilon)\, A_i \bigr) - \beta_{\mathrm{KL}}\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) \right) \right]
\end{equation}
where $\rho_{i,t} = \frac{\pi_\theta(o_{i,t} | q, o_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(o_{i,t} | q, o_{i,<t})}$ is the importance sampling ratio. Standard \textit{GRPO} computes the advantage as:
\begin{equation}
	A_i = \frac{r_i - \mathrm{mean}(\{r_1, \dots, r_G\})}{\mathrm{std}(\{r_1, \dots, r_G\}) + \epsilon}
\end{equation}
With $\beta_{\mathrm{KL}} = 0$, the KL term vanishes and training stability is maintained through the clipping mechanism and the learning rate alone.

\subsubsection{Auxiliary Confidence Loss}
\label{sec:conf_loss}
The model is trained to minimize a joint loss:
\begin{equation}
	L_{\mathrm{total}} = -\,\mathcal{J}(\theta) + \beta_{\mathrm{conf}}\, L_{\mathrm{conf}}
\end{equation}
where $\beta_{\mathrm{conf}}$ controls the magnitude of the confidence objective. The confidence loss $L_{\mathrm{conf}}$ uses Binary Cross Entropy (\textit{BCE}) between the dense confidence estimates and the broadcasted ground-truth binary reward $r$:
\begin{equation}
	L_{\mathrm{conf}} = -\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \bigl[r \log(c_t) + (1{-}r)\log(1{-}c_t)\bigr]
\end{equation}

To prevent mode collapse, we apply dynamic per-group importance sampling weights. In complex reasoning tasks, the policy initially generates predominantly incorrect solutions, biasing an unweighted loss toward trivially predicting failure. We compute weights $w_{\mathrm{pos}}$ and $w_{\mathrm{neg}}$ for each group of $G$ generations:
\begin{equation}
	w_{\mathrm{pos}} = \frac{G}{2\, N_{\mathrm{pos}}}, \quad w_{\mathrm{neg}} = \frac{G}{2\, N_{\mathrm{neg}}}
\end{equation}
(set to $0$ if the count is $0$), ensuring balanced contributions of correct and incorrect examples. The reweighted group loss is:
\begin{equation}
	L_{\mathrm{conf}}^{\mathrm{group}} = \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} w_{r_i} \left( -\frac{1}{T_i} \sum_{t=1}^{T_i} \bigl[r_i \log(c_{i,t}) + (1{-}r_i)\log(1{-}c_{i,t})\bigr] \right)
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Sensitivity of the Confidence Loss Factor.}
\label{sec:conf_loss_sensitivity}
The coefficient $\beta_{\mathrm{conf}}$ must be tuned with extreme care. When set an order of magnitude higher than the chosen value (e.g., $0.1$), the gradient contribution of the confidence loss dominates the \textit{GRPO} policy gradient, directing the model's optimization primarily toward confidence prediction rather than answer quality. This leads to \textit{policy destruction}: the reasoning policy collapses, all generated answers become incorrect, and a trivial constant classifier (always predicting ``incorrect'') achieves zero \textit{BCE} loss. With $\beta_{\mathrm{KL}} = 0$, no KL-divergence regularization counteracts this drift---had $\beta_{\mathrm{KL}}$ been nonzero, the penalty for deviating from the base policy would have provided a partial safeguard. Conversely, a too low $\beta_{\mathrm{conf}}$ might lead to poor binary classification performance, as the gradients do not have enough weight in the loss reduction direction of the binary classification task. Gratefully, the signs of policy destruction (reward dropping to zero, confidence loss becoming trivially zero) are detectable very early in training, enabling rapid parameter adjustment. We note that a gradient accumulation factor $K_{\mathrm{grad}} = 4$ was used to simulate a larger effective batch size on a single GPU, which also stabilizes gradients.

\subsubsection{Self-Reward Integration (Advantage Blending)}
\label{sec:advantage_blending}
A key innovation is blending the external sparse reward with the internal dense confidence \citep{laser}. Standard \textit{RLVR} (Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards) assigns binary rewards of $0$ or $1$---the model either produces the correct answer by chance or receives no learning signal. A dense intrinsic reward helps differentiate between \textit{completely wrong} and \textit{almost correct} trajectories, where both would receive $r=0$ from the external verifier. This is particularly powerful for questions at the frontier of the model's capabilities.

In the Self-Rewarding variant, the blended advantage $A^{\mathrm{blended}}_i$ for trajectory $i$ is:
\begin{equation}
	A^{\mathrm{blended}}_i =
	\begin{cases}
		(1 - \alpha_{\mathrm{blend}}) \dfrac{r_i - \mu_r}{\sigma_r + \epsilon} + \alpha_{\mathrm{blend}} \dfrac{\bar{c}_i - \mu_{\bar{c}}}{\sigma_{\bar{c}} + \epsilon} & \text{if } \sigma_{\bar{c}} > \delta_{\mathrm{min}} \\[12pt]
		\dfrac{r_i - \mu_r}{\sigma_r + \epsilon}                                                                                                                         & \text{otherwise}
	\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{c}_i = \frac{1}{T_i}\sum_{t=1}^{T_i} c_{i,t}$ is the mean confidence of trajectory $i$, $\alpha_{\mathrm{blend}}$ is the blending factor, and $\delta_{\mathrm{min}}$ gates the intrinsic reward contribution. When the model's confidence standard deviation across the group is below $\delta_{\mathrm{min}}$ (i.e., equal uncertainty about all samples), the intrinsic signal is suppressed to prevent optimization toward noise---a safeguard adopted from \citet{laser}.

\paragraph{Warmup Phase.}
The validity of the intrinsic signal depends on the model's ability to accurately estimate confidence. For the Self-Rewarding variant, we implement a long warmup of $N_{\mathrm{warmup}}$ processed samples---chosen to correspond approximately to the first half of the training epoch---during which $\alpha_{\mathrm{blend}}$ is effectively zero. This ensures the confidence head has developed sufficient discriminative accuracy before its signal influences policy optimization. Without this warmup, a poorly calibrated confidence signal would introduce noise that hinders rather than helps training.

\subsection{Dataset and Preprocessing}
\label{sec:dataset}
We utilize the \texttt{\textit{GSM8K}} (Grade School Math 8K) dataset \citep{gsm8k}, a widely-used benchmark for evaluating the mathematical reasoning capabilities of language models. The dataset comprises grade-school-level math word problems requiring multi-step arithmetic reasoning. The full training split contains $7{,}473$ samples. After length filtering, the effective training set was $7{,}012$ samples, and the evaluation set was $512$ samples.

We apply the following preprocessing:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Prompt Formatting.} Each problem is wrapped in the chat template of the target \textit{LLM} (\textit{user} and \textit{system} roles). The system message instructs the model to reason step by step and enclose the final answer in a \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}} command. The last boxed expression is extracted as the prediction for the \texttt{math-verify} library, thus avoiding any stochastic reward model.
	\item \textbf{Length Filtering.} Prompts exceeding $T_{\mathrm{prompt}}$ tokens (including the system message) are removed to ensure all generations fit within the compute budget. This filter is applied identically to training and evaluation splits and is the reason why the effective training set size is $N_{\mathrm{train}} < 7{,}473$: there were not $8{,}192$ samples available after length filtering.
	\item \textbf{Answer Extraction.} For \texttt{\textit{GSM8K}}, the ground-truth answer is extracted from the dataset's annotation format (\texttt{\#\#\#\# <answer>}).
\end{itemize}
The maximum generation length is restricted to $T_{\mathrm{gen}}$ tokens.

\subsection{Experimental Variants}
\label{sec:variants}
To ensure scientific rigor, we define four controlled settings, configured via environment variables:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{BASE (\texttt{base}):} The untouched base model without any fine-tuning, serving as a lower-bound reference. No confidence training is performed.
	\item \textbf{\textit{GRPO} (\texttt{-l-r}):} Standard \textit{RLVR} training with \textit{GRPO}. No confidence loss, no confidence reward. Confidence token embedding training is disabled (the environment variable \texttt{USE\_CONFIDENCE\_LOSS} is set to $0$).
	\item \textbf{Auxiliary-Loss-Only (\texttt{+l-r}):} RL training augmented with the auxiliary confidence loss $\beta_{\mathrm{conf}} L_{\mathrm{conf}}$. The model is optimized for both the RL objective and confidence prediction, but the predicted confidence is \textit{not} used in the advantage function (the environment variable \texttt{USE\_CONFIDENCE\_REWARD} is set to $0$). Confidence token embedding training is enabled.
	\item \textbf{Self-Rewarding (\texttt{+l+r}):} Full pipeline: RL training + auxiliary confidence loss + self-reward integration via advantage blending. No additional environment variable changes beyond the defaults. Confidence token embedding training is enabled.
\end{itemize}
All training runs use the same mode (\texttt{MODE=LOCAL}), the same hyperparameters (see \autoref{app:hyperparams}), and the same data splits.

\subsection{Hardware and Compute}
\label{sec:hardware}
Each training run was carried out on a single \textbf{NVIDIA Quadro GV100} GPU with $5{,}120$ CUDA cores, $32$ GB HBM2 memory, and a peak FP32 performance of $14.8$ TFLOPS. We thank the \textit{JKU Institute for Machine Learning} for providing the student GPU server that was used for training. All computations used the \texttt{float32} data type. Training of one epoch required approximately $13$ hours per variant. Evaluation of the full evaluation dataset ($N_{\mathrm{eval}}$ samples) took approximately $2$ hours per run (one pre-training evaluation for the base model and one post-training evaluation per variant). During training, GPU memory utilization was approximately $29$ GB, and average compute utilization was approximately $50\%$. These numbers are reported for reproducibility, as most publications omit such practical details.

\subsection{Software Stack and Tooling}
\label{sec:software}
At the time of execution, all Python and library dependencies were set to the latest available versions. The complete project configuration is reproduced in \autoref{app:pyproject}.

\textbf{Experiment Tracking.} We used \textit{Weights \& Biases} (\textit{WANDB}) to log all training metrics, loss curves, and generated completions. This tooling was instrumental for debugging training dynamics and iteratively tuning hyperparameters to stable configurations, such as identifying the critical sensitivity of $\beta_{\mathrm{conf}}$ described in \autoref{sec:conf_loss_sensitivity}.

\subsubsection{Note on \textit{vLLM}}
\label{sec:vllm}
We chose not to use \texttt{\textit{vLLM}} for inference acceleration, despite its potential throughput benefits. A primary motivation for this decision was the desire to work with the newest tools and library versions: \texttt{\textit{vLLM}} is very slow in getting its dependencies updated, and at the time of execution, it was incompatible with the library versions listed in \autoref{app:pyproject}. Additionally, \texttt{\textit{vLLM}} lacks full support for Metal Performance Shaders (\textit{MPS}) required for local debugging on macOS. While the \texttt{LogitsProcessor} interface of \texttt{\textit{vLLM}} could in principle be used to extract confidence logits during rollout generation, no gradient flow is possible through the inference engine, necessitating a separate PyTorch forward pass for training in any case.

\paragraph{Effective \textit{RLVR} Training Pipeline.}
All three trained variants demonstrate a substantial and consistent improvement over the untrained BASE model: \textit{Pass@1} increases from $31.35\%$ to approximately $53\%$ (a relative improvement of $\approx 70\%$), and \textit{Pass@4} increases from $59.18\%$ to approximately $73$--$75\%$. This confirms that the \textit{RLVR} training pipeline is effective across all experimental conditions, including those augmented with the auxiliary confidence objective.

\paragraph{\textit{GRPO} vs.\ Confidence Variants.}
The pure \textit{GRPO} variant achieves marginally higher \textit{Pass@1} ($53.27\%$) than the Auxiliary-Loss-Only ($52.73\%$) and Self-Rewarding ($52.59\%$) variants. This is expected: the auxiliary confidence loss consumes a fraction of the model's limited capacity ($0.5$B parameters, $1.75\%$ trainable via \textit{LoRA}), diverting some gradient signal from the primary reasoning objective. However, the difference is small ($< 1$ percentage point), indicating that the multi-task learning setup imposes only a negligible cost on answer quality.

\paragraph{Near-Zero Truncation.}
Across all variants, the truncation rate is below $0.25\%$, meaning that virtually all generated completions terminated naturally within the $T_{\mathrm{gen}}$ token budget. This validates the adequacy of the generation length setting for the \texttt{\textit{GSM8K}} domain.

\paragraph{Confidence Token Never Sampled.}
Critically, the confidence token inclusion percentage is exactly $0.0\%$ for \textit{every} variant, including those with trained confidence. This empirically validates the theoretical argument of \autoref{sec:interference}: the sigmoid-based logit formulation, combined with the naturally low initial logit magnitude of the untrained token, prevents the confidence token from ever reaching the sampling threshold. Both issues identified in the original \textit{LaSeR} paper \citep{laser}---the necessity of a non-standard additional forward pass and the risk of confidence token sampling---are resolved by this approach.

\paragraph{Strong Classification Performance.}
The confidence-trained variants achieve remarkably good binary classification performance, considering the severe capacity constraints of a $0.5$B parameter model with only $1.75\%$ of parameters trainable. The Self-Rewarding variant (\texttt{+l+r}) attains an \textit{MCC} of $0.58$ and a balanced accuracy of $77.89\%$, while the Auxiliary-Loss-Only variant (\texttt{+l-r}) achieves an \textit{MCC} of $0.49$ and a balanced accuracy of $74.03\%$.

\paragraph{Consistent Improvement of \texttt{+l+r} over \texttt{+l-r}.}
Across every binary classification metric, the Self-Rewarding variant outperforms the Auxiliary-Loss-Only variant. We hypothesize that this improvement arises from a virtuous cycle: by integrating the confidence signal into the advantage function, the Self-Rewarding variant preferentially reinforces trajectories that the model can predict accurately. This creates a selection pressure toward solutions whose correctness the model can reliably assess, thereby improving both the policy and the confidence calibration simultaneously. In contrast, the Auxiliary-Loss-Only variant trains the confidence head on whatever trajectories the policy produces, without feedback between confidence accuracy and trajectory selection.

\paragraph{Harmonic Balanced Accuracy.}
We note that the metric termed ``F1 score'' in the original \textit{LaSeR} paper \citep{laser} corresponds to what the implementation measures as \textit{harmonic balanced accuracy}---the harmonic mean of sensitivity (TPR) and specificity (TNR)---rather than the standard F1 score (harmonic mean of precision and recall). In our evaluation, we report both metrics explicitly. The harmonic balanced accuracy of $76.29\%$ (\texttt{+l+r}) confirms a well-balanced classifier that performs adequately on both positive and negative classes.

\paragraph{Binary Classification vs.\ Regression.}
Our binary classification framing (via the sigmoid and \textit{BCE} loss with threshold $0.5$) is conceptually cleaner than the continuous MSE-based reward regression used in \textit{LaSeR} \citep{laser}. The binary approach naturally aligns with the reward structure of verifiable reasoning tasks, where correctness is inherently binary, and enables the use of well-established classification diagnostics. The high ROC AUC ($87.88\%$ for \texttt{+l+r}) indicates strong discriminative ability across all threshold choices.

\subsection{Confidence-Guided Answer Selection}
\label{sec:confidence_selection}
When investing additional test-time compute by generating $G$ candidate answers per prompt, confidence-guided selection strategies become relevant:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Highest Confidence:} Select the answer with the highest mean confidence score. The Auxiliary-Loss-Only variant achieves $60.16\%$ via this method---substantially higher than \textit{GRPO}'s $52.54\%$---demonstrating that a well-trained confidence head can effectively identify the most likely correct answer among multiple candidates.
	\item \textbf{Confidence-Weighted Majority Voting:} Weight each answer's vote by its confidence score. This yields $60.35\%$ for \texttt{+l-r} and $59.57\%$ for \texttt{+l+r}, compared to $59.57\%$ for unweighted \textit{GRPO} majority voting.
\end{itemize}

These results already show modest improvements over plain majority voting, even with the limited self-assessment capabilities of a $0.5$B model. With stronger confidence calibration in larger \textit{LLMs} ($100$+ billion parameters), confidence-guided selection could yield substantially larger gains, particularly when scaling the number of generations $G$ at test time.

\subsection{Additional Observations}
\label{sec:observations}

\paragraph{Answer Length Reduction.}
We observed that the generated answers for the Auxiliary-Loss-Only variant (\texttt{+l-r}) were approximately $25\%$ shorter than those of all other variants. While we cannot provide a definitive causal explanation, one tentative hypothesis is that the multi-task nature of simultaneously learning to predict confidence incentivizes more concise reasoning: shorter, more decisive chains may be easier for the model to assess confidently, creating an implicit regularization effect. Perhaps the confidence loss is easier to compute for shorter concise answers than longer convoluted ones. Without the self-reward component (which incentivizes higher absolute confidence), the model has no pressure to produce longer reasoning to justify high confidence scores, allowing it to converge on more compact representations.

\paragraph{Dense Reward Signal and Scaling Potential.}
A fundamental advantage of the Self-Rewarding variant (\texttt{+l+r}) is that the model does not face a sparse binary reward of $0$ or $1$ alone, but additionally receives a \textit{continuous} self-score blended into the advantage estimate. In standard \textit{RLVR} with binary rewards, the model must discover correct answers essentially by chance to receive any positive signal---for difficult problems, this can lead to training stagnation. The dense self-reward provides a gradient signal that differentiates between ``almost correct'' and ``completely wrong'' trajectories, even when both receive $r=0$ from the external verifier.

We posit that this mechanism becomes especially powerful at the scale of $100$+ billion parameter models, where the language model's capacity for self-assessment is orders of magnitude greater. In coding domains, for instance, unit tests as \textit{RLVR} signals only provide binary pass/fail, yet a dense self-reward could guide the model through partial solutions step by step. As the old saying in machine learning holds: given enough data and a big enough model in AI, success is guaranteed. It would be valuable to evaluate how the Dense Confidence approach scales to the frontier model category ($100$B to $1{,}000$+B parameters) employed by leading AI companies like \textit{OpenAI}.

\paragraph{Achieveability with Minimal Resources.}
These results were obtained after training for only $N_{\mathrm{epochs}}$ epoch(s), on a relatively small dataset of $N_{\mathrm{train}}$ samples, using a very small $0.5$B parameter model with only $1.75\%$ of parameters trainable via \textit{LoRA}. The consistent improvements across all variants suggest that the approach is robust even under severe resource constraints, and should scale favorably to larger models with more data and training duration. The number of rollouts per prompt ($G$) was set to the lower practical limit; increasing $G$ to $8$ or more would likely improve results substantially, as \textit{GRPO}'s advantage estimation benefits from larger group statistics.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\subsection{Technical Limitations}
A primary constraint is the $0.5$B parameter proxy model, which bounds the upper limit of reasoning complexity and potentially masks emergent self-assessment behaviors observable only at larger scales. Training was restricted to a single epoch to prevent overfitting, deviating from the original \textit{LaSeR} protocol \citep{laser} which trained for two epochs. Additionally, the use of \texttt{\textit{vLLM}} for accelerated inference was precluded by its dependency version lag, as discussed in \autoref{sec:vllm}.

The sensitivity of $\beta_{\mathrm{conf}}$ represents a practical challenge: the risk of policy destruction necessitates careful hyperparameter search and monitoring via tools such as \textit{Weights \& Biases}. Future work could explore adaptive scheduling of $\beta_{\mathrm{conf}}$ or multi-objective optimization techniques to mitigate this fragility.

\subsection{Conclusion and Future Work}
This study validates the feasibility of \textbf{Dense Confidence} as a mechanism for self-supervised process oversight. We demonstrate that even a $0.5$B parameter \textit{LLM}, fine-tuned with \textit{LoRA} adapters on a single consumer-grade GPU, can function as a concurrent binary classifier of its own reasoning correctness, achieving an \textit{MCC} of $0.58$ with negligible impact on answer quality. The critical methodological contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Logit-Based Confidence:} Formulating the intrinsic reward on raw logits rather than softmax probabilities, permanently resolving the confidence-token sampling interference reported in \textit{LaSeR} \citep{laser}.
	\item \textbf{Confidence Token Embedding Training:} Leveraging weight tying between the embedding and output projection layers to provide the most direct optimization path for aligning the confidence token's logit.
	\item \textbf{Dense Trajectory-Wide Monitoring:} Extracting confidence at every generation step, eliminating the need for a separate forward pass and enabling trajectory-wide self-assessment.
\end{enumerate}

Future research should investigate (i)~scaling to frontier-class models to evaluate the dense reward hypothesis at higher capacity, (ii)~training explicit Process Reward Models (\textit{PRM}) \citep{verifystepbystep} from the dense confidence signal, potentially eliminating the dependency on costly human annotations, and (iii)~applying the framework to coding tasks where unit-test-based \textit{RLVR} provides only binary signals.
