+\subsection{Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning with LoRA}
+\label{sec:lora}
+We employ Low-Rank Adaptation (\textit{LoRA}) \citep{lora} to reduce the number of trainable parameters. \textit{LoRA} decomposes the weight update $\Delta W$ of a pre-trained weight matrix $W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$ into two low-rank matrices $A \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r_{\mathrm{LoRA}}}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{r_{\mathrm{LoRA}} \times k}$, such that the adapted forward pass computes $h = W_0 x + \frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{LoRA}}}{r_{\mathrm{LoRA}}} B A x$, where $r_{\mathrm{LoRA}} \ll \min(d,k)$ is the rank and $\alpha_{\mathrm{LoRA}}$ is a scaling factor. This reduces the trainable parameters from $d \times k$ to $(d + k) \times r_{\mathrm{LoRA}}$ per adapted layer.

+\subsubsection{Confidence Token Embedding Training}
+\label{sec:embedding_training}
+A critical design decision is the explicit full-finetuning of the confidence token's embedding vector, enabled via the \texttt{trainable\_token\_indices} mechanism of the \textit{LoRA} configuration. -	\item \textbf{Value Range and Boundedness:} The \textit{LaSeR} formulation $r_{\text{laser}}$ depends on $\log \pi_\theta$. As $\pi_\theta \to 0$, $r_{\text{laser}} \to -\infty$. Our formulation maps confidence to a strictly bounded interval $[0, 1]$ via the sigmoid function, ensuring that the intrinsic reward signal remains within a controlled magnitude, consistent with the scale of the external binary rewards ($0$ or $1$).
+	\item \textbf{Value Range and Boundedness.} The \textit{LaSeR} formulation depends on $\log \pi_\theta$, which diverges as $\pi_\theta \to 0$. -	\item \textbf{Granularity:} The \textit{LaSeR} implementation provides a sparse intrinsic signal, computing the self-reward via an additional forward pass executed after the generation concludes to predict the confidence token at the sequence terminus. In contrast, our dense approach evaluates the confidence token at every generation step $t$ during the initial rollout, aggregating a trajectory-wide belief. +	\item \textbf{Granularity.} The \textit{LaSeR} implementation provides a sparse intrinsic signal via a separate forward pass at the sequence terminus. Our dense approach evaluates the confidence token at every generation step $t$, providing a trajectory-wide assessment of the reasoning chain.
 
-	\item \textbf{Optimization Landscape:} Maximizing the log-probability (\textit{LaSeR}) is consistent with maximum likelihood estimation, pushing the model to eagerly assign mass to the confidence token. Maximizing the sigmoid of the logit (our method) is a softer objective that saturates as the logit increases, potentially preventing overconfidence where the model pushes logits to infinity to satisfy a logarithmic objective.
+	\item \textbf{Gradient Properties and Sampling Interference.} In the \textit{LaSeR} formulation, maximizing $\log\pi_\theta(z_c|x,y)$ is equivalent to maximizing the softmax probability of the confidence token. At sufficient logit magnitude, the confidence token dominates the softmax distribution and is sampled during generation, corrupting the reasoning trace. This interference was identified as a principal challenge in the original \textit{LaSeR} paper \citep{laser}.
 
-	\item \textbf{Interference Avoidance via Gradient Saturation:} A critical structural constraint of our dense monitoring approach is that the designated confidence token must strictly remain latent. In the original \textit{LaSeR} formulation, high confidence logits are unproblematic because the confidence estimation occurs only after the generation is complete (either as an appended token or a separate forward pass), preventing any interference with the reasoning trajectory. In contrast, since we evaluate this token at every step $t$, if its logit $l_t^{\text{conf}}$ were to increase sufficiently to rival the top-$k$ tokens, it would be sampled during generation, corrupting the logical reasoning trace. Our scaled sigmoid formulation inherently prevents this pathology. Unlike the logarithmic objective which implies a constant gradient drive towards maximizing probability (pushing logits towards $+\infty$), the sigmoid function saturates rapidly once $l_t^{\text{conf}}$ marginally exceeds the effective range defined by $\mu_{\text{conf}}$ and $\sigma_{\text{conf}}$. Upon saturation ($c_t \approx 1$), the gradient vanishes, effectively arresting the upward drift of the logit at a value that remains numerically subordinate to the active vocabulary, thereby guaranteeing that the self-monitoring process remains non-intrusive.
-\end{enumerate}
+	      Our sigmoid formulation operates on the raw logit \textit{before} softmax computation. The initial logit of the untrained confidence token is empirically centered at $\mu_{\mathrm{conf}}$, which is so small relative to the vocabulary tokens that it is irrelevant for sampling. The centering parameter $\mu_{\mathrm{conf}}$ can be shifted further in the negative direction, and because the output projection layer shares its weights with the embedding layer, the model rapidly adapts to the new center.  \subsubsection{Auxiliary Confidence Loss}
-The model is trained to minimize a joint loss: $L_{\text{total}} = - \mathcal{J}(\theta) + \beta_{\text{conf}} L_{\text{conf}}$, where the auxiliary coefficient $\beta_{\text{conf}}$ controls the magnitude of the confidence objective.
-The confidence loss $L_{\text{conf}}$ acts as a regularizer, forcing the confidence scores to predict the eventual correctness of the answer. However, the coefficient $\beta_{\text{conf}}$ must not be set too high, to avoid destroying the pre-trained reasoning policy of the base model. We use Binary Cross Entropy (\textit{BCE}) between the dense confidence estimates and the broadcasted final reward $r$:

 \begin{equation}
-	L_{\text{conf}} = \text{BCE}(\mathbf{c}, \mathbf{r}) = - \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} [r \log(c_t) + (1-r) \log(1-c_t)]
+	L_{\mathrm{total}} = -\,\mathcal{J}(\theta) + \beta_{\mathrm{conf}}\, L_{\mathrm{conf}}

-where $r_i \in \{0, 1\}$ is the ground truth binary reward for trajectory $i$, and $w_{r_i}$ is the class weight corresponding to the label $r_i$. +\paragraph{Sensitivity of the Confidence Loss Factor.}
+\label{sec:conf_loss_sensitivity}
+The coefficient $\beta_{\mathrm{conf}}$ must be tuned with extreme care. When set an order of magnitude higher than the chosen value, the gradient contribution of the confidence loss dominates the GRPO policy gradient, directing the model's optimization primarily toward confidence prediction rather than answer quality. This leads to \textit{policy destruction}: the reasoning policy collapses, all generated answers become incorrect, and a trivial constant classifier (always predicting ``incorrect'') achieves zero BCE loss. With $\beta_{\mathrm{KL}} = 0$, no KL-divergence regularization counteracts this drift---had $\beta_{\mathrm{KL}}$ been nonzero, the penalty for deviating from the base policy would have provided a partial safeguard. Gratefully, the signs of policy destruction (reward dropping to zero, confidence loss becoming trivially zero) are detectable very early in training, enabling rapid parameter adjustment. We note that a gradient accumulation factor $K_{\mathrm{grad}}$ was used to simulate a larger effective batch size on a single GPU, which also stabilizes gradients.
 
 \subsubsection{Self-Reward Integration (Advantage Blending)}
-A key innovation is blending the external sparse reward with the internal dense confidence \citep{laser}. Standard RL struggles when the reward signal is sparse, taking \textbf{only} values of $0$ or $1$ as is common with mathematical verifiers. In this setting, the agent either stumbles upon a solution by chance for difficult questions or learns nothing. A dense reward helps the algorithm differentiate between \textit{completely wrong} and \textit{almost correct} trajectories, whereas both would receive a $0$ reward from the external verifier. This dense signal facilitates iterative improvements, particularly for questions at the frontier of the model's capabilities, provided the self-verification mechanism is sufficiently accurate.
-
-In the \textit{Self-Rewarding} variant, we blend the standard advantage with a standardized confidence score. The blended advantage $A^{\text{blended}}_i$ for trajectory $i$ is defined as:
+\label{sec:advantage_blending}
+A key innovation is blending the external sparse reward with the internal dense confidence \citep{laser}. Standard RLVR (Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards) assigns binary rewards of $0$ or $1$---the model either produces the correct answer by chance or receives no learning signal. A dense intrinsic reward helps differentiate between \textit{completely wrong} and \textit{almost correct} trajectories, where both would receive $r=0$ from the external verifier. This is particularly powerful for questions at the frontier of the model's capabilities.
 
+In the Self-Rewarding variant, the blended advantage $A^{\mathrm{blended}}_i$ for trajectory $i$ is:
 \begin{equation}
-	A^{\text{blended}}_i =
+	A^{\mathrm{blended}}_i =

-		(1 - \alpha) \displaystyle\frac{r_i - \mu_r}{\sigma_r + \epsilon} + \alpha \displaystyle\frac{\bar{c}_i - \mu_{\bar{c}}}{\sigma_{\bar{c}} + \epsilon} & \text{if } \sigma_{\bar{c}} > \delta \\[12pt]
-		\displaystyle\frac{r_i - \mu_r}{\sigma_r + \epsilon}                                                                                                  & \text{otherwise}
+		(1 - \alpha_{\mathrm{blend}}) \dfrac{r_i - \mu_r}{\sigma_r + \epsilon} + \alpha_{\mathrm{blend}} \dfrac{\bar{c}_i - \mu_{\bar{c}}}{\sigma_{\bar{c}} + \epsilon} & \text{if } \sigma_{\bar{c}} > \delta_{\mathrm{min}} \\[12pt]

 	\end{cases}
 \end{equation}
+where $\bar{c}_i = \frac{1}{T_i}\sum_{t=1}^{T_i} c_{i,t}$ is the mean confidence of trajectory $i$, $\alpha_{\mathrm{blend}}$ is the blending factor, and $\delta_{\mathrm{min}}$ gates the intrinsic reward contribution. When the model's confidence standard deviation across the group is below $\delta_{\mathrm{min}}$ (i.e., equal uncertainty about all samples), the intrinsic signal is suppressed to prevent optimization toward noise---a safeguard adopted from \citet{laser}.
 
-where $\bar{c}_i = \frac{1}{T_i} \sum_{t=1}^{T_i} c_{i,t}$ is the mean confidence of trajectory $i$, $\alpha=0.1$ is the blending factor, $\mu_r$ and $\sigma_r$ denote the mean and standard deviation of ground-truth rewards within the group, $\mu_{\bar{c}}$ and $\sigma_{\bar{c}}$ denote the mean and standard deviation of mean confidences within the group, and the threshold $\delta = 0.1$ gates the intrinsic reward contribution. This formulation ensures that intrinsic rewards only influence the policy when the model exhibits sufficient discriminative variance in its confidence estimates across the group. If the model is equally uncertain about all samples in the group (i.e., $\sigma_{\bar{c}} \leq \delta$), we default to the external reward to prevent the RL algorithm from fitting to noise.

-Crucially, as established in the original \textit{LaSeR} protocol \citep{laser}, the validity of this intrinsic signal depends on the model's ability to accurately estimate confidence. To prevent the policy from optimizing towards maximizing a random or poorly calibrated signal early in training, we implement a \textit{warmup phase}. During these initial steps, the blending factor is effectively forced to $\alpha=0$, meaning the policy updates rely exclusively on the sparse ground-truth rewards from the external verifier. The intrinsic confidence signal is only integrated into the advantage function once the auxiliary loss indicates that the internal confidence estimation has stabilized. This is implemented using a configurable warmup period of several optimizer steps that must be executed before activating the blended advantages.
+\paragraph{Warmup Phase.}
+The validity of the intrinsic signal depends on the model's ability to accurately estimate confidence. For the Self-Rewarding variant, we implement a long warmup of $N_{\mathrm{warmup}}$ processed samples---chosen to correspond approximately to the first half of the training epoch---during which $\alpha_{\mathrm{blend}}$ is effectively zero. This ensures the confidence head has developed sufficient discriminative accuracy before its signal influences policy optimization. Without this warmup, a poorly calibrated confidence signal would introduce noise that hinders rather than helps training.
 
 \subsection{Dataset and Preprocessing}
-We utilize the \texttt{Keven16/LaSeR\_training\_data} (specifically the \texttt{DeepMath-103K} subset \citep{deepmath103k}). The dataset consists of math problems requiring step-by-step reasoning. In contrast to the original \textit{LaSeR} study \citep{laser} which utilized the full dataset, we apply specific constraints to accommodate local hardware limitations:
+\label{sec:dataset}
+We utilize the \texttt{GSM8K} (Grade School Math 8K) dataset \citep{gsm8k}, a widely-used benchmark for evaluating the mathematical reasoning capabilities of language models. The dataset comprises grade-school-level math word problems requiring multi-step arithmetic reasoning. The full training split contains $7{,}473$ samples.
+
+We apply the following preprocessing:
 \begin{itemize}
-	\item \textbf{Filtering:} Rows are filtered based on the limited amount of completion tokens to ensure the generation fits within the compute budget. These filters are applied identically to both the training and evaluation sets.
-	\item \textbf{Format:} Prompts are formatted using the chat template for the specific \textit{LLM} (\textit{user} and \textit{system} roles). The system message is particularly important to instruct the model to provide the answer in a format that is processable by the external verifier using deterministic functions. Specifically, the model is directed to enclose the final answer in a \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}} command at the end of the output. This last boxed expression is extracted as the prediction for the \texttt{math-verify} library, thus avoiding the need for a stochastic reward model. The maximum length for the prompt (including system message) is restricted to $128$ tokens, and the maximum generation length is limited to $1024$ tokens.
+	\item \textbf{Prompt Formatting.} Each problem is wrapped in the chat template of the target \textit{LLM} (\textit{user} and \textit{system} roles). The system message instructs the model to reason step by step and enclose the final answer in a \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}} command. The last boxed expression is extracted as the prediction for the \texttt{math-verify} library, thus avoiding any stochastic reward model.
+	\item \textbf{Length Filtering.} Prompts exceeding $T_{\mathrm{prompt}}$ tokens (including the system message) are removed to ensure all generations fit within the compute budget. This filter is applied identically to training and evaluation splits and is the reason why the effective training set size is $N_{\mathrm{train}} < 7{,}473$: there were not $8{,}192$ samples available after length filtering.
+	\item \textbf{Answer Extraction.} For \texttt{GSM8K}, the ground-truth answer is extracted from the dataset's annotation format (\texttt{\#\#\#\# <answer>}).
 \end{itemize}
+The maximum generation length is restricted to $T_{\mathrm{gen}}$ tokens.

-To ensure scientific rigor, we define four controlled settings:

 \subsection{Hardware and Compute}

Total training time per variant was restricted to a single epoch. This deviates from the original \textit{LaSeR} protocol \citep{laser}, which trained for two epochs. We restricted training to one epoch to align with the reduced dataset size and prevent overfitting on the proxy model.
+\label{sec:hardware}
+Each training run was carried out on a single \textbf{NVIDIA Quadro GV100} GPU with $5{,}120$ CUDA cores, $32$ GB HBM2 memory, and a peak FP32 performance of $14.8$ TFLOPS. All computations used the \texttt{float32} data type. Training of one epoch required approximately $13$ hours per variant. Evaluation of the full evaluation dataset ($N_{\mathrm{eval}}$ samples) took approximately $2$ hours per run (one pre-training evaluation for the base model and one post-training evaluation per variant). During training, GPU memory utilization was approximately $29$ GB, and average compute utilization was approximately $50\%$. These numbers are reported for reproducibility, as most publications omit such practical details.

+\subsection{Confidence-Guided Answer Selection}
+\label{sec:confidence_selection}
+When investing additional test-time compute by generating $G$ candidate answers per prompt, confidence-guided selection strategies become relevant:
+
+\begin{itemize}
+	\item \textbf{Highest Confidence:} Select the answer with the highest mean confidence score. The Auxiliary-Loss-Only variant achieves $60.16\%$ via this method---substantially higher than GRPO's $52.54\%$---demonstrating that a well-trained confidence head can effectively identify the most likely correct answer among multiple candidates.
+	\item \textbf{Confidence-Weighted Majority Voting:} Weight each answer's vote by its confidence score. This yields $60.35\%$ for \texttt{+l-r} and $59.57\%$ for \texttt{+l+r}, compared to $59.57\%$ for unweighted GRPO majority voting.
+\end{itemize}
+
+These results already show modest improvements over plain majority voting, even with the limited self-assessment capabilities of a $0.5$B model. With stronger confidence calibration in larger \textit{LLMs} ($100$+ billion parameters), confidence-guided selection could yield substantially larger gains, particularly when scaling the number of generations $G$ at test time.

+\subsection{Additional Observations}
+\label{sec:observations}
+
+\paragraph{Answer Length Reduction.}
+We observed that the generated answers for the Auxiliary-Loss-Only variant (\texttt{+l-r}) were approximately $25\%$ shorter than those of all other variants. While we cannot provide a definitive causal explanation, one tentative hypothesis is that the multi-task nature of simultaneously learning to predict confidence incentivizes more concise reasoning: shorter, more decisive chains may be easier for the model to assess confidently, creating an implicit regularization effect. Without the self-reward component (which incentivizes higher absolute confidence), the model has no pressure to produce longer reasoning to justify high confidence scores, allowing it to converge on more compact representations.
+
+\paragraph{Dense Reward Signal and Scaling Potential.}
+A fundamental advantage of the Self-Rewarding variant (\texttt{+l+r}) is that the model does not face a sparse binary reward of $0$ or $1$ alone, but additionally receives a \textit{continuous} self-score blended into the advantage estimate. In standard RLVR with binary rewards, the model must discover correct answers essentially by chance to receive any positive signal---for difficult problems, this can lead to training stagnation. The dense self-reward provides a gradient signal that differentiates between ``almost correct'' and ``completely wrong'' trajectories, even when both receive $r=0$ from the external verifier.
+
+We posit that this mechanism becomes especially powerful at the scale of $100$+ billion parameter models, where the language model's capacity for self-assessment is orders of magnitude greater. In coding domains, for instance, unit tests as RLVR signals only provide binary pass/fail, yet a dense self-reward could guide the model through partial solutions step by step. As the classic observation in machine learning holds: given sufficient data and model capacity, success is not a question of \textit{if} but \textit{when}. It would be valuable to evaluate how the Dense Confidence approach scales to the frontier model category ($100$B to $1{,}000$+B parameters) employed by leading AI laboratories.
+
+\paragraph{Achieveability with Minimal Resources.}
+These results were obtained after training for only $N_{\mathrm{epochs}}$ epoch(s), on a relatively small dataset of $N_{\mathrm{train}}$ samples, using a very small $0.5$B parameter model with only $1.75\%$ of parameters trainable via \textit{LoRA}. The consistent improvements across all variants suggest that the approach is robust even under severe resource constraints, and should scale favorably to larger models with more data and training duration. The number of rollouts per prompt ($G$) was set to the lower practical limit; increasing $G$ to $8$ or more would likely improve results substantially, as \textit{GRPO}'s advantage estimation benefits from larger group statistics.

 \subsection{Technical Limitations}
-A primary constraint of this study is the computational intensity inherent to \textit{LLM} fine-tuning. We implemented the training loop in pure PyTorch rather than using high-throughput libraries like \texttt{vllm} for the entire pipeline, as the latter currently lack full support for Metal Performance Shaders (\textit{MPS}) required for local debugging. However, for efficient data collection, we could utilize the specific interface \texttt{LogitsProcessor} from the \texttt{vllm} library to modify the logits during the rollout generation. While this accelerates the generation phase, \texttt{vllm} supports only this initial inference step. No gradient flow is possible through the engine. Consequently, the training pass necessitates a separate PyTorch forward pass to capture gradients for the confidence auxiliary loss, introducing complexity in synchronizing state between the inference engine and the training loop. Finally, the reliance on a $0.5$B parameter proxy model bounds the upper limit of reasoning complexity, potentially masking emergent behaviors observable only at larger scales.
+A primary constraint is the $0.5$B parameter proxy model, which bounds the upper limit of reasoning complexity and potentially masks emergent self-assessment behaviors observable only at larger scales. Training was restricted to a single epoch to prevent overfitting, deviating from the original \textit{LaSeR} protocol \citep{laser} which trained for two epochs. Additionally, the use of \texttt{vLLM} for accelerated inference was precluded by its dependency version lag, as discussed in \autoref{sec:vllm}.

+The sensitivity of $\beta_{\mathrm{conf}}$ represents a practical challenge: the risk of policy destruction necessitates careful hyperparameter search and monitoring via tools such as \textit{Weights \& Biases}. +\subsection{Conclusion and Future Work}
+This study validates the feasibility of \textbf{Dense Confidence} as a mechanism for self-supervised process oversight. We demonstrate that even a $0.5$B parameter \textit{LLM}, fine-tuned with \textit{LoRA} adapters on a single consumer-grade GPU, can function as a concurrent binary classifier of its own reasoning correctness, achieving an MCC of $0.58$ with negligible impact on answer quality. The critical methodological contributions are:
+
+\begin{enumerate}
+	\item \textbf{Logit-Based Confidence:} Formulating the intrinsic reward on raw logits rather than softmax probabilities, permanently resolving the confidence-token sampling interference reported in \textit{LaSeR} \citep{laser}.
+	\item \textbf{Confidence Token Embedding Training:} Leveraging weight tying between the embedding and output projection layers to provide the most direct optimization path for aligning the confidence token's logit.
+	\item \textbf{Dense Trajectory-Wide Monitoring:} Extracting confidence at every generation step, eliminating the need for a separate forward pass and enabling trajectory-wide self-assessment.
+\end{enumerate}
+
+Future research should investigate (i)~scaling to frontier-class models to evaluate the dense reward hypothesis at higher capacity, (ii)~training explicit Process Reward Models (\textit{PRM}) \citep{verifystepbystep} from the dense confidence signal, potentially eliminating the dependency on costly human annotations, and (iii)~applying the framework to coding tasks where unit-test-based RLVR provides only binary signals.
